## Postulates, split

### P1 — Anything to do with susceptibility has to do with bacteria
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS for antimicrobial susceptibility, BREAKS as a general rule.** In the laboratory sense, "susceptibility" almost always means bacterial susceptibility: whether a bacterium is killed or stopped by a drug, measured as a minimum inhibitory concentration, and reported as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant. That is bacterial through and through. But susceptibility has a second, host sense that is not bacterial at all: a person's susceptibility to infection — immune state, age, underlying disease, genetics, prior exposure — decides whether an exposure becomes a sickness. Both senses are real, and collapsing them into one loses the host half.
- **Simply put:** in the lab, susceptibility is the bacterium's answer to the drug. In the body, susceptibility is also the body's answer to the bacterium.

### P2 — Medicine reacts with the pests
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS in two separate lanes, BREAKS if the lanes are merged.** Medicines do react with pests in two distinct ways: antibiotics and antifungals react with bacteria and fungi by attacking their walls, ribosomes, and metabolism; antiparasitic and insecticidal medicines react with animal pests — worms, mites, lice, and the insects themselves. What a medicine does not do is react with a pest *as* a bacterium: an antibiotic does not kill a tick, and an insecticide does not kill a bacterium. The pest lane and the bacterium lane are separate treatments for separate targets.
- **Simply put:** one medicine for the bug on the skin, another for the bacterium in the blood — different targets, different drugs.

### P3 — The pests create the bacteria
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: BREAKS as creation, HOLDS as carriage and transmission.** Pests do not create bacteria. A tick does not manufacture *Borrelia*, a mosquito does not manufacture a virus, and a fly does not manufacture a bacterium. What pests do is carry and transmit: they pick an organism up from one host, keep it alive — sometimes multiplying it, sometimes merely transporting it — and deliver it to the next host by bite, by contamination, or by waste. Creation is the wrong arrow; transmission is the right one, and the distinction decides where prevention works: you cannot stop a pest from creating something it never created, but you can stop it from delivering something it carries.
- **Simply put:** the pest is the delivery truck, not the factory.

### P4 — Medicine also reacts with other wastes
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS, and this is the part most often missed.** Bacteria produce wastes that medicine must reckon with even after the bacterium itself is dealt with: toxins secreted into tissue, endotoxin released when a Gram-negative cell wall breaks apart, biofilm slime that shields a colony from drugs, and metabolic byproducts that inflame surrounding tissue. A drug can kill the bacterium and still leave its waste behind to cause sickness — which is why killing the organism and treating the syndrome are two jobs, not one, as in the Nov 3, 2026 funnel theory.
- **Simply put:** kill the factory and you still have to clean up what it dumped.

## Combined statement (his thought + the record)
His chain holds if "create" is read as "carry and deliver": susceptibility in the lab is bacterial, medicine meets the pest and the bacterium on separate lanes, the pest delivers the organism rather than making it, and the organism's wastes are a second target that outlasts the organism itself. What breaks is only the creation arrow — and fixing that arrow strengthens his point rather than weakening it, because a delivery system can be interrupted, while a creation system cannot.
