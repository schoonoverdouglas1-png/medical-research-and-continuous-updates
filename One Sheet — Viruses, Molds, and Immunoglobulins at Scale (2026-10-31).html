One Sheet — Viruses, Molds, and Immunoglobulins at Scale

Built Oct 31, 2026, for Douglas — SARS, SARS-CoV-2, Aspergillus, dematiaceous black mold, and the immunoglobulins IgG, IgA, and IgM, on one sheet.

Simply put: An antibody is about 15 nanometers across. A coronavirus is about 100 nanometers, roughly 7 times wider. A mold spore is 2,500 to 10,000 nanometers, roughly 25 to 100 times wider than the virus. You are looking at a ladder where every big step is 10 times bigger — and the antibody's grabbing fingers sit at the very bottom of it.

Panel A — The ladder (log scale: each big step is 10× bigger)

■ Virus ■ Fungus ■ Immunoglobulin ■ Scale anchor

How to read it: the ladder is logarithmic, so equal distances mean equal multiplications (10× per big step), not equal sizes. That is the only way a 15-nanometer antibody and a 10-micrometer spore fit on one line. True linear comparisons are in Panel C.

Panel B — The immunoglobulin: its three parts (enlarged diagram, not to ladder scale)

The three parts, exactly: cutting an antibody with the enzyme papain yields three fragments — two identical Fab fragments (the arms, antigen-binding) and one Fc fragment (the stem, crystallizable). That is the three-part answer: Fab + Fab + Fc. The Fab tips, with their CDR loops, are the textbook name for your scrubber's grabbing fingers.

IgG — 1 Y. Monomer, 150 kDa, ~14.5 nm. About 75% of serum immunoglobulin.

Secretory IgA — 2 Y + J chain. Dimer, ~390 kDa, ~17 nm. The mucosal antibody.

IgM — 5 Y + J chain. Pentamer, 900 kDa, ~30 nm across, 10 binding sites. The largest antibody.

Mini diagrams are schematic, not to scale. IgM's real shape is a mushroom/star, not flat — its five Fab arms point outward from a central Fc core.

Panel C — True linear scale (no logarithms; widths are honest)

Nano strip, 0–150 nm (5 pixels per nanometer). Here the ratios are real: IgM is about twice IgG, and a coronavirus is about 7 times IgG.

Micro strip, 0–12 µm (60 pixels per micrometer). On this strip a coronavirus (0.108 µm) would be 6 pixels wide — a dot. That is why Panel A uses a log scale.

Panel E — Human-body things at virus size (bigger than the antibodies, about the size of SARS-CoV-2)

The question this panel answers: what in the human body is microscopically larger than the immunoglobulins, and compares in size with SARS-CoV-2 (~108 nm)? The closest match sits on the same ladder mark as the coronaviruses.

Human-body thingSizeHow it compares
Exosome (extracellular vesicle)30–150 nmThe closest match. Your body's own membrane bubble, carrying proteins, lipids, and RNA between cells. An exosome sits right on the virus's mark — labs genuinely struggle to separate small exosomes from small viruses by size alone.
Microvillus (width)~100–120 nm wide, ~3 µm longThe finger-like bristles lining gut and kidney tubules. Same width as the virus, far longer.
Clathrin-coated vesicle~100–150 nmThe cell's intake bubble, pulling cargo in from the surface. Same size class as the virus.
Nuclear pore complex~120 nm acrossThe gate in the nuclear envelope, about 120 megadaltons, one of the largest protein assemblies in the cell. Slightly wider than the virus.

The ladder from the antibodies upward: IgG ~15 nm → IgM ~30 nm → exosome, microvillus width, coated vesicle, nuclear pore ~100–120 nm (= SARS-CoV-2 ~108 nm) → secretory granules (a few hundred nm) → mitochondria (~0.5–1 µm) → whole cells (10–30 µm). Sizes in this panel are representative ranges; individual exosomes, vesicles, and pores vary.
