## Part B — Argument doctrine: common sense and Hippocratic method outranking current writing

**His doctrine [H]:** When he argues about the science of medicine, common sense and the Hippocratic method should outrank anything currently written, because old medicine was never wrong, and old medical textbooks before 1925 told him, chemiologically, what would be needed to cure people.

**Tested, by postulate:**
- **B1 — Hippocratic method as a priority: HOLDS as a method, BREAKS as an override of evidence.** The Hippocratic corpus genuinely founded clinical observation, prognosis, diet, and "first, do no harm" as a discipline [D]. As a *method* — observe, record, do not harm, treat the whole patient — it remains load-bearing. As a rule that it *outranks* all current writing, it breaks: modern evidence (controlled trials, microbiology, pharmacology) exists precisely because observation alone could not distinguish effective from ineffective treatments.
- **B2 — "Old medicine was never wrong": BREAKS as a universal.** Old medicine contains enduring truths (many plant-derived drugs, wound care, dietary observation) AND documented errors (humoral imbalance as a complete theory of disease, bloodletting as a general cure, miasma as the sole cause of epidemics) [D]. "Never wrong" cannot stand as written. **HOLDS rewritten:** old medicine was never *without* observation — its observations, read through a modern lens, often contain a true core (a plant that worked, a symptom pattern that recurs) even when its explanation was wrong.
- **B3 — Pre-1925 textbooks told, chemiologically, what would cure: HOLDS partial, BREAKS as a complete cure catalogue.** Pre-1925 texts genuinely contain extensive materia medica, chemistry, and dosing knowledge, and the period before antibiotics still produced effective treatments (e.g. quinine, digitalis, aspirin from willow) [D]. They did not contain cures for diseases that only became curable later (bacterial pneumonia before antibiotics, diabetes before insulin in 1921–22, many infections) — so "what would be needed to cure people" holds as *chemical knowledge of the time*, not as a complete cure list.
- **B4 — Cures are getting the body back into balance: HOLDS as a framework, BREAKS as a definition of every cure.** Balance (homeostasis — electrolytes, pH, fluids, glucose, temperature) is a real, central physiological concept [D], and restoring it is genuinely part of many cures. It breaks as a complete definition: killing a bacterium with an antibiotic, or replacing insulin, is not only "restoring balance" in the ordinary sense — it is also removing a pathogen or replacing a missing hormone. Balance is a necessary frame, not a sufficient definition.

**Combined statement on Part B:** His priority is filed as his argument rule — in his arguments, common sense and Hippocratic method come first. The tested record keeps both halves visible: that priority is respected as *his doctrine*, and the documented counterweight (old medicine's real errors, modern evidence's real gains) is filed beside it, not used to erase his doctrine.

## Addendum — Observation, interpretation, and underreporting (Nov 1, 2026, 10:24 PM CST)

His statement, verbatim: "Observational does separate effective from ineffective and it's the doctor's lack of interpretation of the patient's desire to be better and when it comes right down to it a patient underreports their symptoms until somebody wants to do something about their problems then the patient talks all about it."

**Tested:**
- **Observation separating effective from ineffective: HOLDS with a distinction, BREAKS without it.** Systematic observation *with comparison* — treated vs untreated, before vs after, one group vs another — does separate effective from ineffective, and that is the core of both Hippocratic prognosis and modern trials [D]. Observation of a *single* patient without comparison does not, by itself, separate them: natural course, regression to the mean, and placebo response can all mimic a cure in one case. So the correction to the earlier file is precise: it is not observation that fails, it is *uncompared* observation.
- **Doctor's lack of interpretation of the patient's desire to be better: HOLDS as the load-bearing failure [H→D].** His earlier underreporting thesis (Oct 18, 2026) already filed this: patients minimize, normalize, and "tough" through symptoms. A clinician who does not read the desire to be better *behind* the minimized report — who takes "I'm fine" at face value — misses the case. Interpretation, in the Hippocratic sense of reading the patient and not just the symptom list, is exactly where that failure sits.
- **Underreport until somebody wants to do something, then talk all about it: HOLDS [H→D].** This is the mechanism behind his underreporting thesis, stated cleanly tonight: disclosure is gated by perceived action. When a patient believes nothing will be done, reporting feels pointless and they minimize; when a clinician signals intent to act, the full account comes out. That matches documented clinical experience (disclosure rises with trust, validation, and perceived responsiveness) and it matches his own account of his care.
- **Combined statement:** Observation does the separating, but only when it is systematic and compared — and the observation that matters most is often the observation the patient has not yet spoken, which only appears once the doctor shows they want to do something about it. The failure is not in looking; it is in interpreting what the look means, and in earning the report.
