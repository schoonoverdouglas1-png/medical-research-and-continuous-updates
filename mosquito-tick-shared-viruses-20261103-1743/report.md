# Mosquito–Tick Shared Viruses: General Analysis (not medical advice)

## Summary

**Answer in one line: mosquitoes and ticks do NOT, as a rule, carry the same viruses in the same indigenous areas. The major human arboviruses are almost all single-vector-group viruses. A small number of viruses have been *detected* in both mosquitoes and ticks in nature, but no major human pathogen was found with an established, sustained dual mosquito-plus-tick transmission cycle in the same place and time.**

The strongest dual-detection cases are all edge cases, and the report keeps them separate from proven transmission:

- **Field-detected in both, mosquito transmission unproven:** Jingmenviruses — Jingmen tick virus (JMTV) and Alongshan virus (ALSV), family Flaviviridae.
- **Historical field isolation in both, modern cycle unproven:** Batken virus and Dhori virus, genus *Thogotovirus*, family Orthomyxoviridae.
- **Mosquito-primary, tick isolation + laboratory tick competence only:** Sindbis virus (Togaviridae).
- **Mosquito-primary, tick isolation/persistence only, tick transmission weak or absent:** West Nile virus, Bunyamwera virus, Semliki Forest virus, Ndumu virus — the Kenya Ijara tick isolations.
- **Tick-primary or mosquito-primary with only an experimental artefact in the other group:** Powassan virus in *Aedes aegypti* (persisted ~2 weeks, did not multiply), Rift Valley fever virus in inoculated *Hyalomma* ticks (failed when ticks fed on viraemic hosts).

**Regional headline:** In the Cascade Range / Pacific Northwest and in the US Northeast / Great Lakes, mosquitoes and ticks *overlap geographically and seasonally* but carry *different* viruses. Co-exposure to a mosquito virus and a tick virus in the same person (documented for Jamestown Canyon + Lyme, and for Powassan + Jamestown Canyon serology) is not evidence of a shared virus. No source found documents one virus species detected in both a mosquito and a tick in the Cascades, or in the Northeast/Great Lakes, in the same place and time.

Confidence: moderate-high for the control list and the "no established dual cycle" conclusion (CDC classification + multiple experimental studies agree); moderate for Jingmenvirus/Thogotovirus details (recent, partly sequence-only evidence); low for any claim about a specific Cascade locality (surveillance is state-level, not Cascade-specific).

**Tracks kept separate throughout:** virus (species) ≠ vector (mosquito/tick species) ≠ disease (human illness) ≠ test (PCR/isolation in a vector pool, serology in a host, or a clinical test in a patient). Detection in a tick pool, in particular, does not equal transmission by that tick — ticks sampled from a viraemic animal may simply contain a recent blood meal.

**What "established dual-vector cycle" would require (and why none qualifies):** repeated field isolation from both vector groups in the same focus + demonstration that each group can acquire virus by feeding, replicate/disseminate to salivary glands, and transmit by bite to a susceptible host + evidence the cycle is maintained in nature. Jingmenviruses meet the first limb only; Sindbis meets the laboratory limbs only; the Kenya viruses meet the first limb for ticks only. A review cited in the VSV literature makes the same general point: "individual arboviruses are transmitted by a single type of vector … with the exception of Vesicular Stomatitis virus, which is carried and transmitted readily by both sandflies and mosquitoes" — note: sandflies + mosquitoes, **not** ticks; VSV is therefore not added to the mosquito–tick list (see §5).

### 2c. Other regions where both vector groups were actually sampled for the same virus

- **Ijara District, north-eastern Kenya (strongest same-focus field evidence).** Pastoralist/wildlife interface; 10,488 ticks from livestock + wildlife in 1,520 pools (76% *Rhipicephalus pulchellus*, plus *Hyalomma truncatum*, *Amblyomma gemma*, *A. lepidum*). Tick isolates: Bunyamwera 44, Semliki Forest 25, Ndumu 2, WNV 3, Thogoto 3, Dugbe 5 (tick-borne). Mosquito strains of Semliki Forest virus from the same North Eastern Province cluster closely with the tick isolate, and WNV had previously been isolated from Kenyan mosquitoes — so both vector groups carried closely related strains in the same province, but the study did **not** sample mosquitoes and ticks simultaneously at one site/date, and did **not** demonstrate tick-to-host transmission. Grade: same region, probable same transmission milieu, transmission unproven.
- **Yaoundé/Garoua, Cameroon (Jingmenvirus).** Mosquito sampling 2022–2023 (rainy + dry seasons, two biotopes) found HdJV in 4 mosquito species; the near-identical human plasma virus came from the same area. Tick sampling for the same HdJV strain in the same area/time was not reported in the paper's abstract — the tick link is from the wider Jingmenvirus literature (China, Brazil, etc.), not a Cameroon tick–mosquito pair. Grade: same area for mosquito + human, tick link imported from elsewhere.
- **China (ALSV/JMTV) and Russia/Finland/France (ALSV).** Tick detections (*I. persulcatus*, *I. ricinus*) and mosquito detections (*An. yatsushiroensis*, *Ae. vexans*, *Cx. pipiens pallens*, *Cx. tritaeniorhynchus*) are reported for the same virus species but in different studies/years/regions; no single study in sources found reports both vector groups from one focus at one time with a transmission demonstration.
- **Kyrgyzstan (Batken) / Volga estuary, Russia (Dhori).** Historical isolations from both ticks and mosquitoes in the same broad region (1970s), without modern follow-up in sources found.
- **Senegal (Matam + Thiès, Sep 2022–Mar 2024) — a negative control for "overlap".** One integrated study sampled 6,558 mosquitoes (23 spp.) and 1,904 ticks (7 spp.) over two seasons: RVFV was found **exclusively** in *Aedes vexans* mosquitoes (Matam), CCHFV in multiple tick species (both regions, both seasons, *Hyalomma rufipes* predominant in dry season). Same study, same seasons, overlapping geography — **different viruses, strictly segregated by vector group.** Source: https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/tropicalmed/tropicalmed-11-00173/article_deploy/tropicalmed-11-00173-v2.pdf?version=1782985840

**Pattern:** within a single genus, vector group is usually conserved — e.g. *Orthoflavivirus* splits into mosquito-borne (WNV, SLEV, dengue, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis) and tick-borne (TBEV, POWV, louping ill, Kyasanur) sub-groups, with ~50% of flaviviruses mosquito-borne and ~28% tick-borne (ICTV 9th Report, https://ictv.global/report_9th/RNApos/Flaviviridae). Jingmenviruses are the exception that proves the pattern is ecological, not taxonomic law.

## 4. Mechanisms that allow or block dual-vector use

### 4.1 Vector competence is a multi-barrier phenotype, not a single switch
Competence = acquire virus in a blood meal → infect midgut epithelium → escape midgut → disseminate → infect salivary glands → transmit in saliva at the next bite. Failure at any barrier = incompetence, even if virus is detectable in the vector. Sources describe three named mosquito barriers — **midgut infection barrier, midgut escape barrier, salivary gland barrier** — and note both viral and vector genetics influence them. In ticks the picture is less complete: tick salivary glands are grape-like/branched acini (vs lobed mosquito glands), and one review raises the possibility that tick-borne viruses might pass from haemocoel to saliva without infecting salivary glands, which would make the mosquito barrier model an imperfect fit for ticks.

Sources: https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fmicb.2021.773211 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9121816/ ; https://www.clinicalmicrobiologyandinfection.org/article/S1198-743X(14)60438-9/pdf

### 4.2 Entry receptors: conserved, but not sufficient
- Alphaviruses (incl. Sindbis, Semliki Forest, EEE, WEE) use LDL-receptor family members (LDLR, VLDLR, ApoER2) and their invertebrate orthologues in both mammalian and mosquito cells — a receptor conserved across the vertebrate–invertebrate divide, which *permits* broad host use but does not guarantee it.
- Flaviviruses use attachment/entry molecules including C-type lectins, phosphatidylserine (TIM/TAM) receptors, integrins, heat-shock proteins, claudin-1 and LDLR/scavenger receptor B1; heparan-sulfate proteoglycan binding is described as low-affinity, non-specific concentration on the cell surface, not true receptor-mediated entry. Flavivirus E-protein domain III interacts with mosquito midgut surface structures and can determine competence at entry.
- Bunyaviruses: LRP1 facilitates entry of mosquito-borne RVFV and Oropouche virus.
- No source found identifies a tick-specific receptor whose absence blocks mosquito viruses, or vice versa. Receptor presence is therefore best read as *permissive background*; the discriminating steps are downstream (midgut escape, dissemination, salivary glands) and ecological.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12558446/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11462183/ ; https://d.docksci.com/tissue-barriers-to-arbovirus-infection-in-mosquitoes_5a32da99d64ab273f258dac4.html

### 4.3 Temperature and extrinsic incubation
Higher temperature generally shortens the extrinsic incubation period and increases transmission efficiency; in nature, fluctuating temperature alters vector competence. A virus whose incubation exceeds the vector's lifespan or feeding interval cannot be transmitted by that vector, however competent its cells are in vitro. This cuts both ways: mosquitoes live weeks and feed in minutes, so they need fast dissemination; ticks feed for days, live months–years, and can maintain virus transstadially (across moults) and transovarially (to eggs) — which is why a tick-persistent virus like WNV in *Ornithodoros* can look like a reservoir without being an efficient vector.

### 4.4 Host range and viraemia threshold
A vector can only acquire virus if the host it feeds on is viraemic at a sufficient titre, for long enough, and is a host that vector actually bites. Examples: WNV needs viraemic passerine birds for *Culex*; JCV needs white-tailed deer; POWV needs small mammals/woodchucks; CTFV needs ground squirrels/marmots/chipmunks. A virus with a narrow amplifying-host range that one vector group rarely bites will not be picked up by the other group, regardless of cellular susceptibility. Conversely, the Kenya pattern is explained by shared hosts: warthogs, cattle, sheep and goats in one pastoralist system are bitten by both mosquitoes and several tick species, maximising cross-detection. Non-viraemic (co-feeding) transmission — demonstrated for Thogoto virus in ticks and for VSV in blackflies — can bypass the viraemia requirement when infected and uninfected vectors feed close together, but it has not been shown to create a mosquito–tick bridge for any virus in sources found.

### 4.5 Innate immunity: shared toolkit, different weighting
- **RNAi** is described as the most important antiviral response in mosquitoes and "likely" the most important in all arthropods, acting as a midgut gatekeeper and limiting dissemination; but for ticks specifically, reviews state "we know little about the role of RNAi in ticks".
- **JAK/STAT, Toll, Imd** pathways are implicated in insect antiviral defence; in Sindbis-infected ticks, early autophagy then rising apoptosis, plus changes in immune/transport/metabolism genes, were observed.
- **Microbiota:** *Wolbachia* (mosquitoes) competes for cholesterol needed for enveloped virion formation and modulates autophagy; midgut bacteria compete for lipids. Tick microbiomes differ and are less characterised for this purpose.
- Net effect: immunity modulates *efficiency* within a competent pairing; it is not presented in sources as the primary on/off switch between mosquito and tick use. Co-evolution matters more: a virus adapted to suppress/ tolerate one vector's immunity may be cleared or may kill the other vector.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4188209/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9121816/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2024.1334351

### 4.6 Does virus size play a role? — No evidence found that it does
- Sizes overlap completely across the mosquito/tick divide: flaviviruses 40–60 nm (mosquito-borne WNV/dengue and tick-borne TBEV/POWV are the same genus and size class); alphaviruses 60–70 nm (mosquito-borne Sindbis, also tick-isolated); bunyaviruses ~100 nm; thogotoviruses 75–140 nm (tick + mosquito isolated); ALSV ~40.5 nm (both groups detected).
- The one size-related barrier discussed is the mosquito midgut basal lamina, whose pore size is smaller than a virus particle — but that applies to *all* arboviruses in mosquitoes, and crossing it (by direct penetration or anatomical routes) is a property of the virus–vector pair, not of virion diameter ranking mosquito vs tick viruses.
- The "body size" literature is about *vector* body size (e.g. conflicting results for whether smaller vs larger *Aedes triseriatus* transmit La Crosse virus better), not virion size.
- A general "viral size and epidemiology" hypothesis paper correlates size with outbreak frequency/case fatality across all viruses, but does not test mosquito-vs-tick vector assignment and is not evidence for a size mechanism here.
- **Conclusion:** size is not a discriminating mechanism on current evidence. Genome organisation is also not discriminating: dual-detected viruses include non-segmented (+ssRNA flavivirus-like Jingmenviruses, alphaviruses) and segmented (thogotoviruses, bunyaviruses) viruses, and tick-only and mosquito-only viruses occur in both categories.

## 5. Gaps, thin and conflicting evidence — explicitly marked

1. **No established dual mosquito–tick maintenance cycle for any major human virus was found.** Every dual-detection case downgrades to detection-only, historical-only, or laboratory-only on inspection.
2. **Cascades/PNW shared virus: GAP.** No source documents the same virus in both a mosquito and a tick in the Cascades. State-level WA/OR data show different viruses in each vector group. *Ixodes pacificus*–Powassan in the PNW is **unverified** — do not infer it from *I. scapularis*–Powassan in the Northeast.
3. **Northeast/Great Lakes shared virus: GAP.** POWV and JCV co-occur regionally and seasonally and can co-infect a person via two bites, but no source shows JCV in a tick or POWV in a mosquito as a natural cycle.
4. **Jingmenvirus mosquito transmission: UNPROVEN.** Sequence detection in mosquitoes + a near-identical human case in the same area is suggestive, not proof; no mosquito acquisition → dissemination → bite-transmission study was found.
5. **Kenya tick isolates: transmission UNPROVEN.** Isolation from engorged ticks on viraemic hosts cannot distinguish a blood-meal passenger from a replicating, transmissible infection. Authors themselves use "potential role" / "remains unknown".
6. **Thogoto virus mosquito isolate: UNRESOLVED.** Attributed to "one subtype" without species-level resolution in sources found; do not cite Thogoto virus as dual-vector without that qualifier.
7. **Vesicular stomatitis virus: NOT a mosquito–tick case.** It is the cited exception to single-vector-type transmission, but for sandflies + mosquitoes (and blackflies/midges, partly mechanical). Tick mentions in older US outbreak reporting list ticks among "arthropods" generally; no biological tick transmission was verified. Do not add VSV to the shared list.
8. **TBEV in mosquitoes: historical only.** Sporadic isolation from mosquitoes/flies/fleas/lice is noted, with transmission role "not demonstrated"; a 1950 experimental study does not change the classification.
9. **Serology ≠ vector proof.** Antibodies in a host (e.g. Jingmenvirus seroprevalence in China/France, Thogoto/Dhori antibodies in livestock/humans) show exposure, not which arthropod delivered the virus. Likewise, a positive clinical test in a patient does not identify the vector.
10. **Surveillance asymmetry.** Mosquito pools are routinely tested for WNV/EEE/SLEV/JCV (e.g. NJ tested 7,099 pools in 2026); tick testing is usually targeted (Lyme, anaplasmosis, Powassan) and rarely includes mosquito viruses, and vice versa. Absence of detection in the other vector group is therefore weaker evidence than it appears — but the experimental incompetence results (§1, §3) are independent of that asymmetry.
11. **Taxonomy drift.** California serogroup viruses were Bunyaviridae in older sources, Peribunyaviridae now; CTFV was Reoviridae, now Spinareoviridae in current ICTV taxonomy. Family names in older papers should be translated, not quoted as conflicts.

## Could not verify
- Any Cascade-specific (county/elevation-band) mosquito + tick virus survey.
- Mosquito competence for JMTV/ALSV, Batken, Dhori, Bunyamwera, Semliki Forest, Ndumu viruses (no transmission study found).
- Tick competence for any California-serogroup virus, EEEV, WEEV, VEEV, SLEV, dengue, Zika, chikungunya (no positive study found; not the same as a published negative for every pairing).
- A virion-size threshold or mechanism separating mosquito from tick viruses (no source found).
