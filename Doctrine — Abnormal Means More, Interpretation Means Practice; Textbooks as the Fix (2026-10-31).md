# Doctrine — Abnormal Means More, Interpretation Means Practice; Textbooks as the Fix (2026-10-31)

**Dictation:** Douglas, Oct 31, 2026, ~2:36 PM CDT — "When the viruses show in a laboratory test and the laboratory test flags as abnormal, it means that there's more of something there than is supposed to be and when you're laboratory not sick according to laboratory medicine standards, not abnormal, typically means well, abnormal almost always means you're sick and that's why it still takes interpretation but when the computer interprets it better than the average clinician, and then the clinician is given excuses to use against standard science and common sense, it makes it difficult to practice and this is why I'm trying to write textbooks to fix this problem."

**Reading flagged, his to correct:** "when you're laboratory not sick according to laboratory medicine standards, not abnormal" is read as: when the laboratory calls you not sick — i.e., the result is not flagged abnormal — by laboratory standards.

## Postulates

### D1 — An abnormal flag means more of something than is supposed to be there [H→D partial]

**Holds for quantitative tests:** a titer of 1:20 against a <1:10 reference, or an antibody concentration above a line, literally means more antibody than the reference. A viral load above a threshold literally means more virus. For those tests, "more" is not a metaphor.

**Breaks as a universal:** binary tests (positive/negative) do not measure "more" — they measure match versus no-match. And "supposed to be" is the reference range, which is a population statistic, not a personal destiny.
- Simply put: On a test that counts, abnormal means more. On a test that only matches, abnormal means present.

### D2 — Abnormal almost always means you're sick [BREAKS as a rule; HOLDS as a prompt]

**The seams, stated plainly:**
- Reference ranges are conventionally set to cover the middle 95% of healthy people — which means about 5% of healthy people fall outside the range and get flagged abnormal while not sick. Abnormal cannot mean sick by definition, or the range would not be built that way.
- Normal does not mean not sick either: early disease, seronegative disease, and diseases without a lab marker all return normal labs in sick people.
- What abnormal reliably means is: look closer. It is a prompt for interpretation, not a diagnosis.

**His own concession is the honest line:** "that's why it still takes interpretation." The flag starts the question; it does not answer it. That sentence carries the whole doctrine.
- Simply put: Abnormal means "outside the range." Sick is what interpretation decides after that.

### D3 — The computer interprets better, and clinicians are given excuses against science and common sense [H, his view, with a balanced line]

Filed as his view: he experiences clinicians dismissing a clear interpretation with excuses, against standard science and common sense, and that dismissal makes practice difficult.

**The balanced line (mine, labeled):** pattern-matching across large reference sets, consistently and without fatigue, is a computer strength; examination, history, and judgment about the whole patient are clinician strengths that no flag carries. The failure mode he names — a clear pattern dismissed — is a real communication failure. It does not prove either side better; it proves the two sides are not meeting at the flag. His textbooks aim at that meeting point.
- Simply put: The computer reads the pattern; the clinician reads the patient. The fix is getting both readings to the same table.

### D4 — Textbooks as the fix [H, his mission]

This rejoins the origin of the whole project: Dave Cook's charge to "try to save medicine and save the world somehow." The textbooks' job, in this doctrine, is to standardize interpretation — to teach, in both registers (13th-grade treatment and "Simply put" telling), what a flag means, what it does not mean, and what comes next — so that a result cannot be dismissed with an excuse, nor mistaken for a diagnosis. That is the fix the books are for.
- Simply put: Write down how to read the flag, the same way every time, so nobody has to argue about it from scratch.

## Combined statement

An abnormal flag on a counting test means more of something than the range allows; on any test it means look closer, not "you are sick" — because healthy people fall outside ranges and sick people fall inside them. Interpretation is still the load-bearing step, and when a clear pattern gets dismissed instead of met, practice breaks down. The textbooks exist to fix that break: one standard way to read the flag, taught plainly.

**Note on tracks:** he keeps illness, sickness, and disease distinct here — three of his six separate tracks, never collapsed, per his standing rule.

### A1 — Common sense as the standard [H, his standard, honored]

His epistemic standard is stated plainly: from a common-sense perspective, his thoughts have to make sense. That is the same standard his combined-statement endorsement sets — thoughts tested and finished in a way that is not illogical. It is honored here as the method, not just the topic.

### A2 — More, or wrong balance, plus feeling ill [H→D, strengthened by his own qualifier]

The qualifier he adds himself — "by the time you're getting blood tests AND not feeling well" — is what makes this claim strong. Abnormal labs alone do not prove disease (previous section's seams stand). But abnormal labs PLUS symptoms together are the classic case for a diagnosis: the pattern and the complaint pointing the same way.

"Wrong balance" adds a second leg beyond "more," and it has its own foothold: imbalance is itself a disease signal — electrolyte imbalance, acid-base imbalance, and, in his own biome theory, dysbiosis, the wrong balance of the microbial community. Excess and imbalance are two different ways a system shows it is off.

Tightened form: more-than-supposed or wrong-balance, together with feeling ill, means a diagnosis is warranted — a workup owed, not a disease proven. The distinction matters: warranted workup is the claim that holds; proven disease from labs alone is the claim that breaks.

### A3 — Arguing that you are sick [H, his experience and view]

Filed as his experience: a person who feels ill and has abnormal labs should not have to argue that they are sick. Filed as his view: physicians these days argue with themselves to come up with excuses for people either to be sick or not be sick, and it is frustrating either way — the arguing itself is the frustration, whichever direction it runs. No clinician's side is adjudicated here; the frustration is recorded as his.

### A4 — The 100-year standard: old observation plus new medicines [H→D partial]

His proposal: use 100-year-old diagnostic standards — clinical diagnosis by observation and physical examination of the patient — with the medicines of today.

**Where it holds:** history and physical examination remain the foundation of diagnosis even in modern medicine; the bedside method is not obsolete, it is underused. Observation plus examination plus modern therapeutics is a strong complement — old method, new tools, which is his old-with-new rule applied to medicine itself.

**Where it breaks as a complete replacement:** some diseases cannot be seen by observation alone — early cancers, silent infections, lab-defined conditions (a diabetes threshold, a genetic condition) have no bedside showing. The old method is necessary and not sufficient. Tightened form: observe and examine first, test to complete — never test instead of looking.

### A5 — The good-doctor standard [H, his standard]

His standard: a doctor who cannot walk up to a person and fix them, with or without the test, is not a good doctor either way. As an ideal of bedside competence — diagnosis and treatment not wholly dependent on test results — it holds and is worth printing. As a literal standard it overreaches: some conditions cannot be fixed at all, and some cannot even be found without tests. Ideal kept; literal form not claimed.

- Simply put: Look at the patient first, use the test to finish the picture, and don't make a sick person argue that they are sick. Old eyes, new medicines.
