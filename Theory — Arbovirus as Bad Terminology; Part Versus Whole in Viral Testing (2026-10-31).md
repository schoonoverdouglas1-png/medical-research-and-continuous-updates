## Postulates

### P1 — "Arbovirus" as bad terminology [H→D]

**Holds.** "Arbovirus" is not a taxonomic identity; it is an ecological grouping by transmission route — viruses carried by arthropods. It spans multiple viral families (flaviviruses, togaviruses, bunyaviruses among them). The name therefore describes one point of discovery — found via the vector — not the point of origin and not what the virus actually is. That is exactly his three-way distinction, and it matches his standing naming doctrine: a name taken from the route or place of discovery misleads when it is read as identity.

**His "technically misleading but it's not" resolves this way:** misleading as an identity label, not misleading to someone who knows the same virus exists in the vector, in the human, and in other places. The name is a route tag, not a definition.
- Simply put: Arbovirus says how it travels, not what it is.

### P2 — Which tests look at what [D]

| Test | What it actually sees | Part or whole |
|---|---|---|
| PCR (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) | A fragment of viral RNA — conserved targets such as ORF1ab, N, and E — out of a genome of approximately 29,900 nucleotides | Part |
| Antigen test | A viral protein fragment | Part |
| Serology / IFA (his arbopanel) | Antibodies — the blood's response, not the virus at all | Response, not virus |
| Culture | Live, whole virus growing | Whole |
| Whole-genome sequencing | The entire genome, used for surveillance and lineage assignment | Whole |
| Electron microscopy | The whole particle, including its size | Whole + size |

Only culture, sequencing, and microscopy see the whole. Every routine test reads a part or a response. Dual-target PCR (for example ORF1ab combined with N) is recommended so that a mutation in a single primer or probe binding site does not cause a false negative — the field already guards against part-level failure.

### P3 — How misidentification happens [D, and directly relevant to his panel]

**Three mechanisms, all documented:**

1. **A shared fragment matches a relative.** Conserved targets are shared across relatives — the E gene is used as a screening target precisely because of broad pan-sarbecovirus reactivity. A part-level match can point at the family when the question asked for the species.
2. **Antibodies cross-react.** Diagnosis of specific California serogroup viruses is complicated by the high degree of antibody cross-reactivity between them, with laboratory standards requiring a fourfold higher neutralizing-antibody titer to positively identify the etiologic virus — and a fourfold difference is not always observed, so neutralizing titers may not clearly differentiate between different California serogroup viruses. Jamestown Canyon cases have been misdiagnosed as La Crosse virus due to cross-reactivity of neutralizing-antibody responses. This is the misidentification vector inside his own panel: an IFA-level "California (La Crosse)" result is California-serogroup-level evidence, and virus-level identity (La Crosse versus Jamestown Canyon) needs neutralization titers or molecular testing — the same virus-level resolution his Marshfield note describes.
3. **A response is read as a current virus.** IgG means past exposure (his Brown note), not a virus present now. Reading a response as a present particle is the third misconstrual.

**And size:** PCR and serology cannot see size at all — they match sequences or antigens, not diameters. Big versus small is invisible to these tests; only microscopy sizes a particle. So a test cannot tell a big virus from a small one — only match from no-match.
- Simply put: The blood holds the whole reality; the tube holds only a template. A positive is a match to the template, not a photograph of the virus.

### P4 — SARS versus SARS-2, big versus small [BREAKS as a size difference]

SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 sit in the same size class (roughly 80–140 nm; SARS-CoV-2 measured at 108 ± 8 nm in this morning's One Sheet). Their difference is sequence (~20% of the genome different) and behavior, not size. The big-versus-small question is real across the virome — parvoviruses near 20 nm against giant viruses near 750 nm — but it does not split the SARS pair. The Obaid conversations about the SARS difference are filed as his account; the size leg of that difference does not hold.
- Simply put: SARS and SARS-2 are cousins of the same size. Big and small live elsewhere in the virus world, not between those two.

## Combined statement

"Arbovirus" misleads when read as identity, because it names only the route of discovery; the virus itself exists in vector, human, and elsewhere. Routine tests read parts or responses, never the whole virus — PCR a fragment of ~29,900 nucleotides, serology an antibody rather than a particle — and misidentification enters exactly there: shared fragments, cross-reactive antibodies (Jamestown Canyon read as La Crosse), and responses read as present virus. Size, meanwhile, is invisible to those tests altogether.

## Addendum — Does La Crosse Presume Jamestown Canyon? (Oct 31, ~2:24 PM CDT)

**Question:** Douglas, Oct 31, 2026, ~2:24 PM CDT — "It seems like with certain testing standards that it's almost a presumptive assumed that when you have lacrosse encephalitis or lacrosse virus that you also have Jamestown Canyon virus and I'm not certain if that's 100% true or not but if you don't test 100% of the time for both those things you might only find one or the other."

**Short answer:** Not 100% true — and his uncertainty is correct. No standard presumes that having La Crosse means also having Jamestown Canyon. What the standard presumes is the reverse problem.

### Three things that get blurred, separated

1. **Co-infection (both viruses actually present):** possible in principle (same upper-Midwest region, overlapping mosquito seasons), but NOT presumed by any standard. Each would be a separate infection event. No rule says one implies the other.
2. **Cross-reactivity (one infection, antibodies to both):** this is the real presumption in the standards — that a positive for one may BE the other. The CDC Jamestown Canyon algorithm states that simultaneous testing for another California serogroup virus, La Crosse virus, is typically done to distinguish between these closely related viruses【5921675001356726667†L46-L47】. A positive La Crosse IgM result should be confirmed by neutralizing antibody testing because of potential cross-reactivity to related viruses such as Jamestown Canyon【5921675001356726667†L185-L187】. When neutralizing antibodies for both viruses are detected, infection status is classified by titer, with a ≥4-fold higher titer against one virus than the other used to determine the more likely infection【5921675001356726667†L215-L218】.
3. **Ascertainment (you find what you test for):** his second point HOLDS, with proof. Before a Jamestown Canyon-specific IgM test arrived in 2013, JCV cases ran 3–4 per year and were misdiagnosed as La Crosse; after it, 40–70 per year. The virus did not multiply tenfold in 2013 — the testing did. If you do not test for both every time, you will only find the one you tested for.

### The panel-design point (about the test, not about his result)

His arbopanel carries "California (La Crosse)" as a single analyte with no separate Jamestown Canyon analyte. By design, that analyte cannot separate the two — a Jamestown Canyon infection could read through the California analyte by cross-reactivity, or be missed as its own virus. No health interpretation is made here; this is the test's design.

### One more seam — persistence

Jamestown Canyon IgM antibodies can persist for more than a year, and JCV neutralizing antibodies are found in 4%–54% of asymptomatic persons in endemic regions【5921675001356726667†L206-L210】. So even a Jamestown Canyon positive does not by itself prove current disease — timing needs paired samples and the 4-fold rise. The standards presume ambiguity, not co-infection.

- Simply put: The standard does not assume you have both. It assumes one positive might be the other — so it tests both together, and lets the 4-fold titer name the likelier virus.
