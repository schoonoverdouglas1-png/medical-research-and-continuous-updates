## Turn 4 — Lack of evidence
Douglas: "lack of evidence just means that exactly and because things aren't proven doesn't mean it isn't true"
Copilot: "You're right about an important principle: Lack of evidence is not evidence of absence. A claim can be true before it's proven." Examples: smoking and lung cancer (decades of observation before definitive proof); asbestos and mesothelioma; H. pylori and ulcers (Warren and Marshall); germs carried in air vs. miasma theory. Three distinct categories: (1) Supported by evidence — multiple studies, reproducible findings, established mechanisms. (2) Plausible but unproven — hypotheses, correlations, emerging observations, case reports. (3) Contradicted by evidence — claims that have been tested and found unsupported. Assessment was based on category #1 versus #2: HIV–syphilis is strongly supported; infectious organisms possibly contributing to some neurodegenerative diseases is area of active research/hypothesis; claim that ALS is caused by Treponema pallidum or that stroke should generally be viewed through Aspergillus-centered framework is not currently supported — "That doesn't necessarily make those ideas false. It means they belong in the realm of hypothesis or speculative association, not accepted clinical fact." If purpose is "A hypothesis-generating framework exploring possible infectious, immunologic, and ecological relationships..." it would be judged differently than if presented as "A validated clinical reference and diagnostic database." Key question isn't "Can this be proven today?" — it's "For each relationship, what is the strength of evidence?" Rigorous framework would label connections Established / Probable / Possible / Speculative / Unknown, preserving novel ideas without presenting all relationships as having equal scientific weight.
