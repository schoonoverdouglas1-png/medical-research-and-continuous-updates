# Cascade Mountain Range Tick Bites and Tick-Borne Testing — Research Report

**Scope note (from mission):** General research analysis only — not a medical recommendation for any person, no test order, no laboratory contact.
**Tracks kept separate throughout:** tick (vector) ≠ virus/organism ≠ disease/syndrome ≠ test. A positive antibody is evidence of an immune response, not proof the organism is present; a PCR positive is nucleic-acid detection, not by itself a disease diagnosis.

## Summary

- The Cascade human-biting picture is dominated by **hard ticks** *Ixodes pacificus*, *Dermacentor andersoni*, *Dermacentor occidentalis* (mainly California/southern Oregon), and *Dermacentor similis* (western dog tick, formerly western *D. variabilis*), plus the **soft tick** *Ornithodoros hermsi* in high-elevation coniferous forest, especially the eastern Cascade slopes. The only well-established endemic **tick-borne virus** in this picture is **Colorado tick fever virus** (CTFV, *Coltivirus*), carried by *D. andersoni* (and found in *D. occidentalis*).
- **Powassan virus (POWV)** is a real tick-borne flavivirus with a Mayo blood PCR, but it is **not established as a Cascade-endemic human pathogen** in the sources found: its US human cases are concentrated in the Northeast/Great Lakes, and no Washington cases were shown. Do not list it as a Cascade virus without that qualifier.
- **Eastern, Western and Venezuelan equine encephalitis viruses (EEEV, WEEV, VEEV) are mosquito-borne alphaviruses, not Cascade tick-borne viruses.** WEEV is the only one with a genuine western-US history (mosquito *Culex tarsalis* + passerine birds; last Washington human arboviral case cited as WEE in 1988; last US human case in the literature cited as 1994/1999 depending on source — see disagreement note). A tick role for WEEV/VEEV appears only as a possible/overwintering or experimental route in older veterinary summaries, not as an established Cascade tick–mammal cycle.
- **Hypothesis test (Q3): NOT supported as a general rule.** Arboviruses are defined by replicating in *both* the ectothermic arthropod and the homeothermic vertebrate as the *same* virus. Virion size is a property of the viral family, not of the host it was last in. The Cascade tick virus CTFV (~60–80 nm, dsRNA, 12 segments) is actually *larger* than the mosquito-borne alphaviruses (~60–70 nm) and flaviviruses (~50 nm). Temperature changes replication *rate* and the extrinsic incubation period, not viral identity/size. A tick can, however, co-carry and co-transmit *different* organisms — that is co-infection, not one virus turning into another, larger virus in the mammal.
- **Mayo blood PCRs (whole blood EDTA unless noted):** TIKLB (BABPB + EPCRB + BMIPB), PBORB (Lyme), POWVB (Powassan), BRBST (broad-range bacterial 16S PCR+NGS, second-tier only), CBBRP (Q fever). **No Mayo blood PCR was found** for *Rickettsia rickettsii*/*R. philipii* specifically, for *Francisella tularensis* specifically, or for Colorado tick fever virus — those fall to state public-health / CDC–APHL reference routes (see Q4).
- **Arbovirus antibody panel ARBOP (serum, IFA)** covers four *mosquito-borne* encephalitis viruses only: California (La Crosse), EEE, St. Louis, WEE. It covers **none** of the Cascade tick-borne viruses, bacteria or parasites, and it does **not** include VEEV or West Nile virus.

## 1. Tick species indigenous to the Cascades, main human-biting species, and pathogens

### Caveat on “indigenous”
Oregon has ~20 hard-tick species, but only four are commonly found on humans in Oregon: *I. pacificus*, *D. andersoni*, *D. variabilis* (in older literature) and *D. occidentalis* — Oregon State / Oregon Extension summary, https://ehs.oregonstate.edu/sites/ehs.oregonstate.edu/files/2026-07/Tick_and_Tick-Borne_Disease_Awareness%20.pdf and https://digitalcollections.library.oregon.gov/assets/displaypdf/287563 . ## 2. Tick-borne arboviruses vs equine encephalitis viruses — shared or separate?

### 2a. The equine encephalitis alphaviruses (family *Togaviridae*, genus *Alphavirus*)
All three are **mosquito-borne**; wild birds are the main reservoir; horses and humans are generally dead-end hosts for EEE/WEE (insufficient viraemia to infect mosquitoes), whereas equids *are* amplifying hosts for VEE — https://www.dovepress.com/equine-viral-encephalitis-prevalence-impact-and-management-strategies-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-VMRR .

| Virus | Principal vector | Reservoir / amplifying host | Geography & Cascade relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV)** | *Culiseta melanura* (enzootic, freshwater-swamp birds) + bridge *Aedes* spp. | Wild (swamp) birds | Eastern/Gulf Coast US. **Not a Cascade tick virus, not a Cascade mosquito mainstay.** Mayo's ARBOP page itself calls EEE “a low-prevalence cause of human disease in the Eastern and Gulf Coast states” — https://www.mayocliniclabs.com/test-catalog/overview/83267 . |
| **Western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV)** | *Culex tarsalis* (principal), passerine birds | Passerine/domestic birds; small mammals minor | **The western one.** Historically western US incl. eastern WA Yakima Valley outbreaks 1939–1942 (*Cx. tarsalis* shown as primary vector). ### 2b. Is there a tick link at all for EEE/WEE/VEE?
**Only weak/secondary, and it must not be presented as the cycle.** Older veterinary summaries list “other vectors or overwintering hosts” for WEEV as possibly including *Dermacentor andersoni* ticks, the assassin bug *Triatoma sanguisuga* and the cliff-swallow bug, and note epidemic VEEV “may also be transmitted by ticks” — https://en.wikivet.net:443/index.php?title=Equine_Encephalitis_Virus&oldid=104040 — and cite a 1937 paper “The tick as a vector for the virus disease equine encephalomyelitis.” That is **experimental/historical, not an established field cycle** in the Cascades, and no Cascade human tick-bite WEEV/EEEV/VEEV transmission series was found. The established answer is: **different viruses circulate in ticks vs in the mosquito–bird–mammal equine cycle; they overlap only in that both are arboviruses and both can infect mammals (including horses/humans) as dead-end or amplifying hosts.**

### 2c. Documented viruses found in BOTH ticks and mammals (same virus, both hosts)
- **Colorado tick fever virus (CTFV)** — tick: *D. andersoni* (principal), also *D. occidentalis*; mammal: humans, rodents and other mammals; disease: Colorado tick fever (biphasic/saddleback fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia; rarely meningitis/encephalitis). This is the textbook Cascade tick+mammal virus. Genome: dsRNA, 12 segments, non-enveloped *Coltivirus* (family *Spinareoviridae*/*Reoviridae* in older taxonomy).
- **Powassan virus (POWV), lineages 1 and 2 (lineage 2 = deer tick virus)** — tick: *Ixodes* spp. (*I. cookei/marxi* lineage 1; *I. scapularis* lineage 2 in the east); mammal: groundhogs, skunks, squirrels, white-footed mice, humans (dead-end, neuroinvasive disease in a minority). Mayo POWVB page states this explicitly — https://www.mayocliniclabs.com/test-catalog/Overview/617484 . **Cascade qualifier:** POWV human disease is northeastern/Great Lakes-concentrated per CDC; no WA/OR/CA Cascade human case series was found — it is a *national* tick+mammal virus, not a proven Cascade one.
- **Tick-borne encephalitis virus complex** (European/Far Eastern/Siberian TBEV) — tick + mammal in Eurasia; **not** a US Cascade virus. Listed only to prevent conflation with POWV, its North American flavivirus relative.
- For completeness, the mosquito+mammal analogues that share the same dual-host logic but with a mosquito vector: West Nile virus, St. Louis encephalitis virus, La Crosse virus, Jamestown Canyon virus — all infect birds/mammals and mosquitoes, not Cascade ticks as principal vectors.

### 2d. Tick-specific / arthropod-specific viruses (tick only, no vertebrate infection)
- **Arthropod-specific (insect/tick-specific) flaviviruses and related viruses:** defined as reproducing exclusively in arthropods with “no defined ability to infect vertebrates” — e.g. cell-fusing agent virus, Palm Creek virus, Parramatta River virus in the flavivirus literature; and more broadly, insect-specific viruses (ISVs) are “characterised by their incapacity to infect vertebrates” — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458977/ and https://mdpi.com/1999-4915/12/9/964/xml .
- **Jingmenviruses:** a spectrum exists — some are arbo-jingmenviruses infecting *both* arthropods and vertebrates (e.g. Jingmen tick virus), others are arthropod-only groups — https://www.nature.com/articles/s44298-025-00106-z?error=cookies_not_supported&code=508e520a-7d55-4716-9154-290522c15fa0 .
- **GAP:** No named Cascade-specific tick-only virus (i.e. a virus found in Cascade *D. andersoni*/*I. pacificus*/*O. hermsi* and proven unable to infect vertebrates) was identified in this research. Tick virome surveys find many such sequences nationally, but naming one for the Cascades specifically would be speculation.
- **Vertebrate-only contrast:** flaviviruses also include “no-known-vector” (NKV) flaviviruses with no established arthropod vector, and most mammalian viruses are not arboviruses at all. A virus being found in a mammal does not make it tick-borne.

## 3. Hypothesis test: smaller virus in the (cold-blooded) tick, larger/different virus in the (warm-blooded) mammal?

**Verdict: Not supported as a within-bite mechanism. Supported only in the trivial sense that different organisms can co-infect, and in the evolutionary sense that arthropod-specific viruses may be ancestors of dual-host arboviruses.**

### 3a. What arboviruses actually do in two hosts
Arboviruses are *defined* by the dual-host requirement: “Must replicate in both arthropod and vertebrate hosts” and “Must be able to infect and replicate in (cold-blooded) arthropods and (warm-blooded) vertebrates — e.g. dengue grows in *Aedes* at 25–30 °C and humans at 37 °C.” The formal definition is the same: replication in poikilothermic (temperature-varying) arthropods and homeothermic (constant-temperature) vertebrates. After ingestion, the virus must infect midgut epithelium, escape the midgut, disseminate via haemolymph, infect salivary glands, and be shed in saliva — the **extrinsic incubation period (EIP)** — before the next bite can transmit it. In ticks (hemimetabolous, long-lived, feeding at each life stage) there are additional routes with no mosquito parallel: **transstadial** (stage-to-stage) and, for POWV, *Babesia* and *Rickettsia*, **transovarial** (female → eggs) maintenance, plus **co-feeding (non-viraemic) transmission** between ticks feeding close together on the same host.

### 3b. Temperature effects — documented
- “Replication of virus in arthropods is influenced by ambient temperature, and, within optimal limits, increasing temperatures have the effect of shortening the extrinsic incubation period and increasing vector efficiency” (arbovirus textbook summary).
- The relationship is **unimodal (“Goldilocks”)**, not “hotter = always more virus”: too cold slows metabolism/replication; too hot harms vector survival/longevity. Example given in the literature: a virus taking ~14 days to become transmissible at 25 °C might take ~7 days at 30 °C.
- In vertebrates, arboviruses must replicate at 37–39 °C. Experimental WNV passage work notes arboviruses are “additionally required to efficiently replicate at internal temperatures of vertebrate hosts (37–39 °C)” and that exceeding thermal limits in mosquitoes is rare — the constraint is usually vector fitness, not a hard viral temperature ceiling.
- **Insect-specific viruses are the exception that proves the rule:** “In contrast to arboviruses, who can replicate at temperatures up to 42 °C, insect-specific viruses replicate only at ambient temperatures” and an insect-specific bunyavirus's replication was “decreased and even hindered by elevated temperatures in invertebrate cells where arboviral bunyaviruses were not. However, it is not sufficient to just lower the temperature to enable the replication of ISVs in mammalian cells” — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458977/ . So temperature is *one* host-range barrier among several (entry, innate immunity, post-entry blocks), not a size switch.

### 3c. Does size correlate with vector vs host?
**No.** Virion diameter is fixed by family/genus, identical whether the particle came from tick, mosquito or mammal:

| Family / virus | Virion diameter | Genome |
|---|---|---|
| Alphavirus (EEEV, WEEV, VEEV, chikungunya) | **~60–70 nm**, enveloped | (+)ssRNA, ~11.7 kb |
| Flavivirus/Orthoflavivirus (POWV, WNV, SLE, dengue) | **~40–60 nm, typically ~50 nm**, enveloped | (+)ssRNA, ~10.9 kb |
| Coltivirus (CTFV — the Cascade tick virus) | **~60–80 nm**, non-enveloped, icosahedral | dsRNA, **12 segments** |
| Orthobunyavirus (La Crosse / California serogroup) | **~80–120 nm**, enveloped | (−)ssRNA, 3 segments |

Source for sizes: NCBI Medical Microbiology chapter https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/bv.fcgi?rid=mmed.section.2894 . **Therefore the “smaller in tick, larger in mammal” prediction fails on its own example:** the established Cascade tick virus (CTFV, up to ~80 nm) is larger than the equine alphaviruses (~70 nm) and the tick flavivirus POWV (~50 nm). There is no documented process by which a bite converts a small tick virus into a larger mammal virus, or by which the mammal harbours a systematically larger version of the same virus. What *does* change between hosts is: viral titre/load, tissue tropism, duration/persistence (often persistent, low-pathogenicity infection in the vector vs acute, sometimes cytopathic infection in the vertebrate), and immune evasion — not particle diameter or genome identity.

### 3d. What *is* documented that could look like the hypothesis
1. **Co-infection / co-transmission:** One *Ixodes* tick can carry *B. burgdorferi* + *A. phagocytophilum* + *Babesia* spp. + *B. miyamotoi*, and one bite can transmit more than one organism. Those are *different* organisms with different sizes — not one virus changing size.
2. **Evolutionary origin:** ISVs and arboviruses are evolutionarily related; “arboviruses could have been ISVs that through evolution acquired the ability to expand their host-range to also include vertebrates.” That is a phylogenetic hypothesis over evolutionary time, not a per-bite transformation.
3. **Recombination (WEEV):** WEEV is a recombinant of an EEE-like virus and a Sindbis-like virus. Again, an ancient evolutionary event, not a tick-to-mammal size change.
4. **Cross-reactivity / serology confusion:** A positive antibody to one flavivirus or alphavirus can cross-react with a related virus (e.g. EEE↔WEE, SLE↔dengue). That can *look* like “a different virus in the mammal” if antibody results are misread as organism identification — which is exactly why the mission's track separation matters.

APHL Sep 2026 menu: CTF listed under PRNT (test 3170); APHL 2025 shared-service FAQ: “Specimens for molecular testing for … Colorado Tick Fever” go to **CDC**, while most other arbovirus serology goes to Wadsworth — https://aphl.org/docs/default-source/infectious-diseases-documents/vector-borne-diseases/arbovirusreferencecenter_testmenus.pdf?sfvrsn=75ba9d05_2 , https://aphl.org/docs/default-source/infectious-diseases-documents/vector-borne-diseases/arbo_sharedservicepilot_faq.pdf?sfvrsn=4de61982_1 . **Note:** CDC's own page warns “Some tests are not currently available at CDC. Please refer to the Test Directory” — current CTF availability must be confirmed via the state lab at time of need; do not assume CDC always offers it. |
| **Rickettsia rickettsii (RMSF) / Rickettsia philipii (PCTF)** — species-specific blood PCR | **None found** (no Mayo “Rickettsia PCR, blood” test surfaced; TIKLB specificity panel lists *R. rickettsii* only as a non-cross-reacting organism) | Only Mayo blood route found is **BRBST broad-range sequencing** (above, second-tier). Blood-PCR route, if any, would be BRBST or state public-health/CDC select-agent laboratory — **not verified — GAP**. Tularemia is a select agent; handling/routing is restricted — another reason not to assume a routine Mayo PCR exists. |

### 4c. Blood vs CSF discipline
Mission scope excluded spinal fluid. For the record: **ABOPC** is the CSF counterpart of ARBOP (IgG/IgM IFA, cutoffs <1:1 in CSF vs <1:10 in serum), and **POWVC / BMIYC** are CSF PCRs. They must not be substituted for a blood result, and a CSF antibody cannot distinguish intrathecal production from blood contamination/blood–brain barrier breakdown (Mayo's own caution).

## 5. Relation to the standard arbovirus antibody panel

**Panel (serum): ARBOP — Arbovirus Antibody Panel, IgG and IgM, Serum** (IFA; serum 0.5 mL, min 0.15 mL; LOINC 49093-8; Rochester) — https://www.mayocliniclabs.com/test-catalog/overview/83267 (opened). Components, none available separately: **CAVP** (California virus [La Crosse] IgG+IgM, S), **EEEP** (Eastern equine), **STLP** (St. Louis), **WEEP** (Western equine). Reference: IgG and IgM <1:10 for each. IgM reliably detected 1–3 weeks post-onset, peaking then declining within 3 months; a single IgG ≥1:10 indicates exposure, a ≥4-fold rise in paired sera indicates recent infection. **CSF counterpart: ABOPC** (CAVPC/EEPC/STLPC/WEEPC) — https://www.mayocliniclabs.com/test-catalog/overview/83897 (opened).

| Cascade / tick picture item | Covered by ARBOP? | Why / caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **WEEV** | **Yes — antigen present** | The one panel member with a genuine western-US history (see §2a). But a positive WEE IgG/IgM is **antibody, not virus**, and EEE↔WEE cross-reactivity exists (infecting-virus titre typically ≥8-fold higher). Given WEEV's near-disappearance, a positive in a Cascade patient needs PHL/CDC PRNT confirmation and epidemiologic correlation; it does not prove a tick bite caused it. |
| **EEEV** | **Yes — antigen present** | But EEEV is an **eastern mosquito** virus, not a Cascade tick virus. Panel inclusion reflects a national encephalitis panel, not Cascade tick ecology. |
| **St. Louis encephalitis virus (SLEV)** | **Yes — antigen present** | Mosquito (*Culex*) virus, historically western US (Yakima Valley 1939–42; WA case 2023). **Not tick-borne.** Cross-reacts with dengue antigens — travel history needed to attribute. |
| **California (La Crosse) virus** | **Yes — antigen present, as “California (LaCrosse)”** | **Misleading name for this mission:** the panel antigen is La Crosse virus, a **mosquito-borne orthobunyavirus** (California serogroup) whose human disease is concentrated in the Midwest/mid-Atlantic/east, in children. The prototype California encephalitis virus itself was a western mosquito virus (rare human disease since 1943). **Neither is tick-borne**, and “California” in the name does not make it a Cascade tick test. |
| **VEEV** | **No** | Despite the “equine” name, VEEV is **absent** from ARBOP. Do not assume the equine panel covers all equine viruses. |
| **West Nile virus** | **No** (separate testing) | The commonest western mosquito encephalitis virus is not in ARBOP; flavivirus cross-reactivity (e.g. SLE antigen vs dengue/WNV) can confuse IFA interpretation. |
| **Jamestown Canyon virus, snowshoe hare virus** (other California-serogroup mosquito viruses, widespread incl. west) | **No** (not as named antigens; possible cross-reactivity with La Crosse antigen is not a specific result) | **GAP for specificity:** whether ARBOP's La Crosse antigen cross-detects Jamestown Canyon antibody at a reportable level was not verified. |
| **Colorado tick fever virus** | **No** | No CTFV antigen in ARBOP; no Mayo blood PCR either (see §4b). A patient with a Cascade tick bite and a negative ARBOP has **not** been tested for the actual Cascade tick virus. |
| **Powassan virus** | **No** | Separate Mayo tests: POWV IgM ELISA (serum) and POWVB/POWVS PCR. Not in ARBOP despite being a flavivirus like SLE/WNV. |
| **All Cascade tick bacteria/parasites** (Lyme *B. burgdorferi*, *B. miyamotoi*, *B. hermsii*, *A. phagocytophilum*, *Babesia duncani/microti*, *Rickettsia* spp., *F. tularensis*, *C. burnetii*) | **No** | ARBOP is a virus-antibody panel only. These need TIKLB/PBOR B/BRBST/CBBRP (blood PCR) or organism-specific serology (e.g. Lyme 2-tier, TICKS/STICK panels — antibody, not PCR — listed in Mayo's tick menu but outside the blood-PCR question). |
| **Tick paralysis** (toxin, not infection) | **No test** | Diagnosis is clinical + tick removal; no organism/antibody test applies. |

**Bottom line for Q5:** ARBOP is a **mosquito-encephalitis antibody panel**, not a tick panel. Its overlap with the Cascade picture is exactly one historically western virus (WEEV) plus three non-tick viruses included for national encephalitis differential diagnosis. A negative ARBOP does not exclude Cascade tick-borne infection, and a positive ARBOP does not identify a tick, a tick-borne organism, or current infection without paired titres, PRNT, exposure history and clinical correlation.

## Could not verify / Gaps
1. No Mayo blood PCR found for CTFV, *R. rickettsii*, *R. philipii*, *F. tularensis* (species-specific), or dedicated *B. hermsii* — see §4b. BRBST is the only Mayo blood route that could in principle identify the bacterial three, but it is broad-range/second-tier, not species-specific.
2. No Cascade-specific tick-only (arthropod-specific) virus named and verified; no Cascade human POWV case series found (POWV is northeastern/Great Lakes-concentrated).
3. Exact POWVS (serum POWV PCR) ordering details, Mayo Rickettsia-antibody test IDs, and the current CDC CTFV test-directory status were not individually verified — CDC itself warns some CTF tests are “not currently available at CDC.”
4. WEEV “last case” year disagrees across sources (1994 in the genome paper; 1999 in secondary summaries; WA-specific 1988 per WA DOH) — reported as a disagreement, not resolved.
5. Whether ARBOP La Crosse antigen cross-detects Jamestown Canyon virus antibody at a clinically reportable level — not verified.
6. *Ehrlichia* spp. and *B. microti* in TIKLB reflect a national panel, not Cascade endemicity; local prevalence of each TIKLB organism in the Cascades specifically (vs the Pacific Northwest generally) was not quantified from a Cascade-only dataset.
7. *Dermacentor similis* vs *variabilis* taxonomy varies by source vintage; older literature cited here as *D. variabilis* for western specimens should be read as *D. similis* under current taxonomy.
8. No live-browser (research-window) confirmation was performed for every Mayo page; TIKLB, ARBOP, ABOPC, POWVB, WA relapsing-fever, WA arboviral and CDC CTF pages were opened via the index (browser_open); other Mayo pages are index/search results. Test availability can change — re-check the Mayo catalog at time of use.
