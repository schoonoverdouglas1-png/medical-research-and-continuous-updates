# Theory — American Name as One Word Combining Two Latin or Greek Words, Translation or Not (2026-11-02)

*Manuscript reference work only — NEVER medical advice. A naming theory, tested against his own tables.*

## His statement, verbatim (Nov 2, 2026, 12:41 AM CST)

> "I believe that the American name is always at least a one word combination for two words with the Latin and or Greek and or Roman sources of those names. Whether there are a translation."

## Reading the statement — two parsings, both kept

The sentence as dictated is compressed (his 10-thoughts-converging account, Nov 1, 2026, 11:53 PM), so it is read two ways, and both are tested — neither is silently chosen over the other:

- **Reading A — "at least one word":** every American name has *at least* one word, and that word (or phrase) combines two Latin/Greek source words.
- **Reading B — "one word, combining two":** the American name is *itself* one word, made by combining two Latin/Greek source words into a compound.

Reading A is trivially true for any name (a name must have at least one word), so the substance of the belief lives in Reading B — and in the trailing question: *whether there is a translation* — i.e. is the American name a translation of the Latin/Greek, or something else (a transliteration, a borrowing, an unrelated common name)?

## First clarification — Roman = Latin, again

"Latin and or Greek and or Roman" collapses to two sources, per last night's Greek/Roman/Latin file: **Roman is Latin** (the language of Rome). So the source list is **Latin and/or Greek** — two languages, not three. A name can be Latin-only, Greek-only, Latin+Greek mixed (the commonest scientific pattern), or neither (English, French, Italian, eponym — see §3).

## Simply put

A lot of American disease names really are one word glued together from two old words — *leukemia* is white + blood, *meningitis* is membrane + inflammation. But not all of them are, and not all of those are translations. Some are the old word spelled in American letters, some are word-for-word translations, and some are brand-new American names that have nothing to do with the Latin or Greek at all.

## §1. Where Reading B HOLDS — one American word, two classical elements

These are the textbook cases, mostly from his own tables. Each American name is one word, each is built from two classical elements, and the table shows whether it is a translation.

| American name (one word) | Element 1 | Element 2 | Translation? | Source language |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Leukemia** | Greek *leukos* (white) | Greek *haima* (blood) | No — **transliteration/borrowing**, not translation. The American word *is* the Greek compound, spelled in Roman letters. A translation would be "white-blood (disease)." | Greek + Greek |
| **Meningitis** | Greek *meninx* (membrane) | Greek *-itis* (inflammation) | No — borrowed compound. Translation would be "membrane-inflammation." | Greek + Greek |
| **Encephalitis** | Greek *en-* (in) + *kephalē* (head) | Greek *-itis* | No — borrowed compound (three elements, not two — a first crack in "two words"). | Greek |
| **Tuberculosis** | Latin *tuberculum* (little swelling) | Greek *-osis* (condition) | No — borrowed compound, Latin + Greek mixed. | Latin + Greek |
| **Pneumonia** | Greek *pneumōn* (lung) | Greek *-ia* (condition) | No — borrowed compound. | Greek |
| **Hypertension** | Greek *hyper-* (over) | Latin *tensio* (pressure, stretching) | No — borrowed compound, Greek + Latin mixed. | Greek + Latin |
| **Hemoglobin** | Greek *haima* (blood) | Latin *globus* (ball, sphere) / *globin* (protein) | No — borrowed compound. | Greek + Latin |
| **Osteoporosis** | Greek *osteon* (bone) | Greek *poros* (pore, passage) + *-osis* | No — borrowed compound. | Greek |
| **Dermatitis** | Greek *derma* (skin) | Greek *-itis* | No — borrowed compound. | Greek |
| **Cardiomyopathy** | Greek *kardia* (heart) + *mys* (muscle) | Greek *patheia* (suffering) | No — borrowed compound, three elements. | Greek |

Pattern: the scientific American name is usually **not a translation at all** — it is the classical compound itself, carried into American letters. That answers the trailing question for this section: *whether there is a translation — mostly, no; there is a borrowing.*

## §2. Where the American name IS a translation — the calque

A **calque** (loan translation) is a word-for-word translation: each classical element is replaced by its English equivalent, and the combination is rebuilt in English. These are the cases where "whether there is a translation" answers **yes**.

| American name | Classical source, element by element | Translation type |
|---|---|---|
| **White blood cell** | *leukocyte*: *leukos* (white) + *kytos* (cell) | Exact calque: white = leukos, blood cell = the cell of blood (with *blood* added in English). |
| **Red blood cell** | *erythrocyte*: *erythros* (red) + *kytos* (cell) | Exact calque. |
| **High blood pressure** | *hypertension*: *hyper-* (high/over) + *tensio* (pressure) | Calque, in plain words — the American common name *translates* the scientific compound. |
| **Hardening of the arteries** | *arteriosclerosis*: *arteria* + *sklerosis* (hardening) | Calque, paraphrased. |
| **Swelling of the brain** | *encephalitis* / *cerebral edema* | Calque, descriptive — used beside, not instead of, the scientific name. |

So his American-first doctrine has two different mechanisms, and the file keeps them apart: **scientific American names borrow** (leukemia), **common American names translate** (white blood cell, high blood pressure). Both are "American first" — but they are not the same operation.

## §3. Where Reading B BREAKS — counterexamples, one per kind

A belief stated as "always" needs only one counterexample per kind to break as an absolute. Here are four kinds, each with a case from his own material:

**a) Single root, not a combination — BREAKS "combination of two."**
- **Cancer** — Latin *cancer* (crab), one root, no second element. American name = the Latin word itself, not a compound.
- **Fever** — Latin *febris*, one root.
- **Asthma** — Greek *asthma* (panting, gasping), one root.
- **Flu** — short for *influenza* (Italian *influenza*, influence), one root, clipped — and Italian, not Latin/Greek at all.

**b) English compound, not Latin/Greek — BREAKS "Latin and/or Greek source."**
- **Ringworm** — English *ring* + *worm*, describing the ring-shaped rash. Not a worm, not Latin, not Greek — and the American name is actively misleading about the cause (a fungus, per his Oct 5, 2026 itraconazole record). His own material's best counterexample.
- **Chickenpox** — English *chicken* + *pox*, not classical at all.
- **Common cold** — English, descriptive, two words in English (which also breaks Reading B's "one word").

**c) Multi-word American name — BREAKS "one word" (Reading B).**
- **Lou Gehrig's disease** — his own naming doctrine's flagship example: American first, Latin second (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis). Three words in English, eponym + *disease*, no Latin/Greek compound in the American name itself.
- **Common cold, high blood pressure, hardening of the arteries** — all multi-word American names, all translations or descriptions, none one-word compounds.

**d) Acronym or eponym — BREAKS both halves.**
- **AIDS** — acronym (acquired immune deficiency syndrome), English initials, not a classical compound.
- **Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Crohn's disease** — eponyms (a person's name + *disease*), no Latin/Greek elements in the American name at all.

## §4. Verdicts

- **Reading A ("at least one word"): HOLDS trivially.** Every name has at least one word — that half of the statement cannot fail, and therefore cannot carry the belief's weight.
- **Reading B ("one word, combining two Latin/Greek words"): HOLDS as a tendency, BREAKS as "always."** It holds for the great run of scientific American names in his tables — leukemia, meningitis, tuberculosis, pneumonia, hypertension, hemoglobin — where one American word really does combine two (sometimes three) classical elements. It breaks on single roots (cancer, fever, asthma), on English compounds (ringworm, chickenpox), on multi-word names (Lou Gehrig's disease, common cold), and on acronyms and eponyms (AIDS, Alzheimer's).
- **"Whether there is a translation": ANSWERED, split three ways.** (1) Scientific names: usually **no translation — borrowing/transliteration** (leukemia *is* leukos + haima, in Roman letters). (2) Common names: often **yes — calque** (white blood cell, high blood pressure). (3) Unrelated common names: **neither** — a new American description or eponym that does not translate the classical name at all (ringworm, Lou Gehrig's disease, consumption for tuberculosis — the old American name for TB, descriptive of wasting, not a translation of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*).
- **Sharpened statement:** *American scientific names are often one word combining two classical elements, borrowed rather than translated; American common names are often translations, descriptions, or eponyms instead — and the American-first doctrine needs both mechanisms, kept apart, to be true.*

## My addition (labeled mine)

For the booklet, teach the three mechanisms as three doors, all labelled "American first": Door 1, **Borrow** (leukemia — the Greek word, in American letters); Door 2, **Translate** (white blood cell — the Greek word, in English words); Door 3, **Rename** (Lou Gehrig's disease, ringworm — a new American name, with the Latin/Greek beside it, second). Students who can tell which door a name came through will never again ask whether an American name "is" a translation — they will ask *which kind* of American name it is.
