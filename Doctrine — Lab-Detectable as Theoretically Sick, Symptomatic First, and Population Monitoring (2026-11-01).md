# Doctrine — Lab-Detectable as Theoretically Sick, Symptomatic First, and Population Monitoring (2026-11-01)

**Provenance:** Douglas's dictation, Nov 1, 2026, ~1:41 PM CST, filed verbatim below. This is a standards doctrine for his research study / Medical Elements book, not medical advice for any individual person. Who gets treated, in what order, is a clinician / triage decision.
**Shiro:** PENDING — reserved, never invented.
**Readings flagged:** "theoretically sick" — kept as his qualifier; it is doing the work in this doctrine (see P1). "Those items" — read as laboratory-detectable items (viruses, fungi, bacteria, antibodies) from the preceding Candida / showing discussion, his to narrow if he meant a specific panel.

## His dictation, verbatim

> "If those items are laboratory detectable, the person is theoretically sick. And that's where the standards have to get better because we have to definitely fix the people that are symptomatic that complain first and the people that are not complaining will actually most likely get better without medical intervention other than some testing so we can you know monitor the population that way."

## Split postulates

### P1. If laboratory detectable, the person is theoretically sick [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **Showing = theoretical sickness [H→D]:** this is his long-standing showing doctrine (Oct 18, 2026: antibodies/antivirus showing indicate illness; Oct 17 §114: illness/sickness/disease based on symptomology). As a *theoretical* tier — "something is present and showing" — it holds: a detectable item is a showing, and a showing is a reason to look, not to ignore.
2. **Showing ≠ clinical sickness [D]:** asymptomatic seropositivity is documented (seroprevalence studies are built on it, per the Oct 18 answer to his own question); *Candida* in 63.6% of healthy volunteers, anelloviruses lifelong in near-100% of healthy adults, and endogenous retroviral sequences in 100% of human genomes are all laboratory-detectable without clinical sickness. A single tier collapses those cases.
3. **The qualifier saves it [LOGIC]:** "theoretically" is the hinge. Read as "theoretically sick = has a showing that warrants a clinical question," P1 holds. Read as "detectable = clinically sick," it breaks on every commensal above.
**Verdict: HOLDS as a theoretical tier; BREAKS as a clinical diagnosis.**
**Simply put:** If a lab can find it, that's a flag worth asking about — call that "theoretically sick." It doesn't by itself mean you're clinically sick. Lots of healthy people carry detectable things that never make them ill.

### P2. Standards have to get better [H]
**Test:**
1. **The gap is real [D]:** current standards do separate colonization from infection, past exposure (IgG) from acute infection (IgM + 4-fold rise), and detection from causation — but they do so differently by organism, by site, and by lab, and the patient-reported symptom side is often a single checkbox. A standard that names both tiers (theoretical showing + clinical sickness) in every report would be better, and his doctrine is a proposal for exactly that.
2. **What "better" must mean [LOGIC]:** better cannot mean "treat every detection" (that would treat 63% of healthy guts for *Candida* and 100% of adults for anelloviruses) nor "ignore every asymptomatic detection" (that would miss early serious disease). Better means: detection + symptoms + site + trajectory, reported together, every time.
3. **His bother threshold [H→D, Oct 17 §114]:** "sick people who don't feel sick don't seek care; when it bothers enough to be a problem, that's when they need the physician" — and the lateness problem that follows: "by the time symptoms are being described, the physician can't always read back how sick the person was before the complaint began." A better standard must capture the pre-complaint slope, not just the complaint day.
**Verdict: HOLDS — a two-tier reporting standard (showing + clinical) is a genuine improvement proposal.**
**Simply put:** Lab reports should say two things, not one: "we found this" and "does this, plus what you feel, add up to sickness?" Right now they often only say the first.

### P3. Fix the symptomatic people who complain, first — definitely [H]
**Test:**
1. **Triage alignment [D]:** standard triage already prioritizes the symptomatic and the severe — the sickest first in emergency care, the complaining patient first in clinic flow. His rule aligns with triage where "complain" means "presenting with symptoms."
2. **His driven-patient doctrine [H, Nov 1, 2026]:** driven patients who have already asked should be fixed first because they asked. That is a *different* rule from sickest-first (asked-first vs. sickest-first), and the file keeps the difference visible: asked-first gets the willing patient into care faster; sickest-first gets the most endangered patient into care faster. Both cannot be "first" at once — the book must say which queue it means in each chapter.
3. **The definite qualifier [LOGIC]:** "definitely" holds as a moral priority (people in distress, asking for help, should not wait behind the comfortable), and breaks as an absolute queue rule (a silent emergency — silent heart attack, silent sepsis — outranks a loud mild complaint in any triage system).
**Verdict: HOLDS as a moral and clinic-flow priority; BREAKS as an absolute triage rule overriding severity.**
**Simply put:** People who are hurting and asking for help should go first — that's right, and it's mostly how clinics already work. The exception is the silent emergency: someone not complaining can still be in more danger than someone complaining loudly.

### P4. People not complaining will most likely get better without medical intervention [H]
**Test:**
1. **Self-limited illness [D]:** most acute viral infections, mild gastroenteritis, and minor injuries resolve without intervention. For the *complaining-free, mildly ill* group, "most likely get better" holds.
2. **Silent serious disease [D]:** hypertension, early diabetes, latent tuberculosis, HIV, early cancers, and silent cardiac disease do not reliably get better without intervention — they progress silently. "Most likely" across *all* non-complainers breaks, because the non-complaining group mixes the self-limited with the silent-serious.
3. **The conditional save [H→D]:** restated as "non-complainers *with a self-limited, monitored pattern* most likely get better without intervention," it holds — and that is exactly why P5 (testing/monitoring) is not optional decoration but the safety net that separates the two groups.
**Verdict: HOLDS for self-limited patterns; BREAKS universal — silent-serious disease is the counterexample that monitoring must catch.**
**Simply put:** Most people who aren't complaining will get better on their own — colds, stomach bugs, small stuff. But some serious things don't hurt at first and don't get better on their own. That's why "most likely" needs a safety net, not just a shrug.

### P5. Other than some testing, so we can monitor the population that way [H]
**Test:**
1. **Surveillance as medicine [D]:** population testing/monitoring — seroprevalence surveys, wastewater, stool and blood panels, cancer screening — is a documented public-health function. It finds the silent-serious cases P4 would otherwise miss, and it tracks whether a showing is rising or falling in a community.
2. **Testing without intervention, honestly bounded [LOGIC]:** testing alone does not treat, and monitoring alone does not fix a found case — the doctrine needs its second half stated: monitor *in order to* intervene when the pattern turns serious. Monitoring as an endpoint breaks; monitoring as a trigger holds.
3. **Fit with his study [H]:** for his research study, this is the design: symptomatic complainers get fixed first; the non-complaining population gets tested and monitored, so the study learns who gets better alone and who turns serious. That design is coherent, and it is testable.
**Verdict: HOLDS — population testing/monitoring as the safety net and the study design, provided monitoring triggers intervention when warranted.**
**Simply put:** Test the people who aren't complaining, watch the numbers, and jump in when the numbers turn bad. That's how you let most people get better alone without missing the ones who won't.

## My labeled addition (mine, not his)

**Name the two tiers in every report.** Theoretical sickness: "detectable, showing present." Clinical sickness: "detectable + compatible symptoms + site + trajectory." A standard that prints both tiers, every time, would make his P1 true without overtreating, his P3 fair without overriding triage, and his P5 safe without pretending monitoring is treatment. That two-tier label is the "better standard" this doctrine is asking for, stated in one sentence.
