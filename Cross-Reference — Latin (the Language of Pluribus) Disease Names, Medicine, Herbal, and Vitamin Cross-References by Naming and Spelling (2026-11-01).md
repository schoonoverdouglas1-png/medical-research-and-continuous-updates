# Cross-Reference — Latin (the Language of *Pluribus*) Disease Names, Medicine, Herbal, and Vitamin Cross-References by Naming and Spelling (2026-11-01)

*Manuscript reference work only — NEVER medical advice. Naming cross-reference: what the spelling shows, and what it does NOT prove about treatment. A shared root is a naming link, not an efficacy claim.*

## His question, verbatim (Nov 1, 2026, 11:36 PM CST)

> "Take the language of the word pluribus and compare that to the names in that same language and show me the different diseases that can be derived from the medicine or from the medicine cross reference or herbal supplement cross references or vitamin cross references to diseases based upon the naming and spelling of the name."

## 0. The anchor word first

**Pluribus is Latin.** It is the dative/ablative plural of *plus* (more, many): *e pluribus unum* = "out of many, one." So "the language of pluribus" = **Latin**, and the language to compare against is the Latin naming system from tonight's two previous files — Latin grammar, with Greek and other roots Latinized inside it.

Two spelling families carry the "many" idea, and they must not be merged:
- **Latin: pluri- / multi-** — *pluribus* (from many), *multus* (many) + *plex* (fold) = multiple, manifold.
- **Greek: poly-** — *polys* (many). Same meaning, different language, different spelling — *polymyalgia* is Greek, *multiple sclerosis* is Latin. A cross-reference that treats poly- and pluri- as the same root would be a spelling error.

## 1. The rule of this cross-reference

A disease name, a medicine name, and a vitamin/herbal name belong in the same row **only if they share a Latin (or Latinized) root or spelling pattern**, and the row states exactly what that sharing shows:

- **Shows:** the namer saw the same feature (colour, shape, place, number, action) in both.
- **Does NOT show:** that the medicine treats the disease, or that the vitamin cures it. Naming is a clue to history, not a prescription. That line is kept in every row below.

## 2. The master table: Latin root → disease → medicine / herbal / vitamin sharing the root

### A. The "many" family — pluribus itself

| Latin root | Meaning | Disease name(s) | Medicine / herbal / vitamin sharing the root | What the naming shows (and does not) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *pluri-* (from *plus, pluris*) | many | No disease is literally named *pluribus*, but the "many" pattern: **multiple sclerosis** (many scars), **multiple myeloma** (many marrow tumours), **multiple endocrine neoplasia** (many glands) | **Pluripotent** stem cells (able to become many types); **pluriglandular** syndromes (many glands) | The *many* idea crosses disease and cell names by spelling. It does NOT make pluripotent cells a treatment for multiple sclerosis — same root, different things. |
| *multi-* (from *multus*, many) | many | **Multiple sclerosis, multiple myeloma** (above); **multinodular** goitre (many nodules) | **Multivitamin** (many vitamins in one) | A multivitamin shares *multi-* with multiple sclerosis by spelling only. Naming link: yes. Treatment link: no — and the table must never imply one. |
| *poly-* (Greek, for contrast) | many | **Polymyalgia** (many muscles aching), **polycystic** ovary/kidney (many cysts), **polyneuropathy** (many nerves) | **Polysaccharide** (many sugars — tonight's capsule word) | Greek twin of the Latin family. Same meaning, different language — kept in a separate row so the Latin/Greek line stays clean. |

### B. Colour roots — Latin adjectives that name both disease and plant

| Latin root | Meaning | Disease name(s) | Medicine / herbal / vitamin sharing the root | What the naming shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *albus* | white | **Albinism** (lack of pigment — from *albus* via *albinus*); **albuminuria** (white protein in urine, old name) | *Salix alba* (white willow) → **salicylic acid** → aspirin lineage; *albumen* (egg white, protein) | Colour named both a condition and a plant. Willow's whiteness did not make it a treatment for albinism — the link is colour, nothing more. |
| *ruber / rubra* | red | **Rubella** (little red one — the red rash); **rubor** (redness, one of the four classic signs of inflammation) | *Echinacea purpurea* is the purple cousin (see below); **rubefacient** medicines (reddening the skin) | Redness named the disease (rash) and the medicine's action (reddening). |
| *purpureus* | purple | **Purpura** (purple spots under the skin — bleeding into skin) | *Echinacea purpurea* (purple coneflower); *Digitalis purpurea* (purple foxglove) | The same Latin colour word sits in a disease (purple spots) and in two herbal names (purple flowers). Spelling link: exact. Treatment link: none — purple foxglove does not treat purpura. |
| *niger* | black | **Nigricans** in acanthosis nigricans (blackening, dark patches) | *Nigella sativa* (black seed / black cumin) | Blackness named both. *Nigella* is a different plant from the disease's cause — naming, not therapy. |
| *flavus* | yellow | **Flavivirus** genus (yellow — yellow fever virus is its type species; *flavus* via Latinized Greek *flavus*); **jaundice** is the English twin (French *jaune*, yellow) | **Flavin** (yellow pigment in riboflavin = vitamin B2) | Yellow runs through a virus genus, a symptom, and a vitamin's chemistry — one colour, three kinds of name. |

### C. Shape and size roots — the body described in Latin

| Latin root | Meaning | Disease name(s) | Medicine / herbal / vitamin sharing the root | What the naming shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *cancer* | crab | **Cancer** (the tumour's crab-like spread, veins like crab legs — Latin *cancer*, crab) | **Cancrum** in cancrum oris (old name for noma, gangrene of the mouth) | The disease took the animal's Latin name by shape. No herbal "cancer" medicine shares the root by treatment — the root is the shape. |
| *tuberculum* | little swelling, little lump | **Tuberculosis** (the disease of tubercles — tonight's TB file); **tuberculoma** (a tuberculous lump) | **Tuberculin** (the test preparation, from the same root) | Disease and test share the root exactly: the test is named *for* the disease's lump, by design. This is the strongest naming link in the table — a test named after its target. |
| *vulgaris* | common | **Acne vulgaris** (common acne), **verruca vulgaris** (common wart), **lupus vulgaris** (common lupus — cutaneous tuberculosis, old usage) | *Beta vulgaris* (common beet), *Plantago* species with *vulgaris* epithets, **vulgar** in common speech | Tonight's vulgaris file, applied: "common" crosses disease names and plant names by the same adjective. It marks frequency, not severity. |
| *lupus* | wolf | **Lupus** (the facial lesion was said to gnaw like a wolf); **lupus vulgaris** and **lupus erythematosus** (red wolf) | **Lupin** (*Lupinus*, wolf-bean — said to wolf the soil's fertility) | Wolf named both a disease and a plant, for different wolf-like qualities (gnawing vs. devouring). Same Latin word, different metaphor — a warning against reading a shared word as a shared mechanism. |
| *variola / varus* | spotted, varied, pimpled | **Variola** (smallpox — the spotted disease); **varicella** (little variola — chickenpox, named as the smaller spotted one) | **Varicose** veins (varied, knotted — same root family) | Smallpox and chickenpox are linked *by naming* (big spotted one, little spotted one) — and modern virology keeps them apart (different viruses). Naming link: yes. Identity link: no. |
| *biloba* | two-lobed | No disease named *biloba* — but **bilobed** describes organs and lobes in disease anatomy | *Ginkgo biloba* (two-lobed leaves) | A herbal name whose Latin describes a leaf shape that also describes anatomy. The leaf's shape does not treat a bilobed organ. |
| *major / minor* | greater / lesser | **Major** and **minor** thalassaemia (greater/lesser severity); **major** depression (greater, as opposed to minor) | *Plantago major* (greater plantain), *Vinca minor* (lesser periwinkle) | Greater/lesser crosses disease severity and plant size by the same two Latin words. |

### D. Action and condition roots — where medicine and disease names are built the same way

| Latin root / suffix | Meaning | Disease name(s) | Medicine / herbal / vitamin sharing the root | What the naming shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *-itis* (Latinized Greek, used in Latin names) | inflammation | **Arthritis** (joint inflammation), **encephalitis** (brain inflammation), **meningitis** (meninges inflammation) | **Anti-inflammatory** medicines (named *against* the -itis); **-itis** is the target in the drug's name, not its ingredient | The suffix builds disease names and, by negation, drug-class names. |
| *-osis* | condition, process | **Tuberculosis** again (condition of tubercles); **osteoporosis** (porous-bone condition); **neurosis** (old, nerve condition) | **-osis** in drug names is rare — the suffix belongs mostly to diseases, which is itself a finding: medicine names actions, diseases name conditions. | |
| *febris* | fever | **Febrile** seizures, **febrile** illness; **antipyretic** is the Greek twin (against fire) | **Febrifuge** (fever-driving-out — old herbal/medicine class, e.g. willow, cinchona) | Fever named both the disease state and the medicine class *against* it. |
| *dolor* | pain | **Dolor** (pain, one of the four inflammation signs); **dolorous** in old descriptions | **Analgesic** is the Greek twin (without pain); **dol-** survives in brand names (e.g. -dol- in analgesic brands) | Pain's Latin root survives in modern brand spelling even where the generic name is Greek. |
| *morbus* | disease | **Morbus** in old names: *morbus gallicus* (French disease = syphilis, old); **morbidity** (disease rate) | **Morbid** in drug safety language (morbid obesity as a treatment indication) | *Morbus* is the Latin word *for* disease itself — every disease name in this table is, in Latin, a *morbus* of something. |
| *officinalis* | of the workshop / pharmacy | No disease — but **officinalis** marks the plant as the pharmacy's own | *Valeriana officinalis* (valerian), *Calendula officinalis* (marigold), *Melissa officinalis* (lemon balm) — all herbal supplements, all "of the shop" | The herbal cross-reference in its purest form: the Latin epithet *is* the medicine claim — "this is the one the apothecary stocks." Naming as a pharmacy label, not a disease name. |

### E. Vitamin cross-references — where the vitamin name IS the disease name, reversed

This is the strongest section of the cross-reference, because vitamin names were often built *from* the disease they prevent:

| Vitamin | Vitamin name, Latin root | Disease it is named against | What the naming shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin C** | **Ascorbic acid** — *a-* (without, Greek) + *scorbutus* (scurvy, Latinized from Dutch *scheurbuik*) = "against scurvy" | **Scurvy** (scorbutus) | The vitamin's chemical name *contains* the disease's Latin name, negated. Naming link: exact and deliberate — the vitamin was named for the disease it prevents. This is the textbook case of a vitamin↔disease naming cross-reference. |
| **Vitamin B3 (niacin)** | **Nicotinic acid** — from *nicotine* (*Nicotiana*, tobacco, named for Jean Nicot) + *-ic acid*; **niacin** coined to avoid the nicotine association | **Pellagra** (Italian *pelle agra*, rough skin — Latin *pellis*, skin + *ager*, rough field) | The disease's name describes its skin; the vitamin's name describes its chemistry's history. The link is historical (pellagra = niacin deficiency), not spelling — a row where the cross-reference is by *cause*, not by shared root, and the table says so. |
| **Vitamin D** | **Calciferol** — Latin *calx* (lime, calcium) + *ferre* (to bear) = "calcium-bearer"; **cholecalciferol** (bile + calcium-bearer) | **Rickets** (English, origin uncertain) and **osteomalacia** (Greek *osteon* bone + *malakia* softness) | The vitamin's Latin name describes its job (bearing calcium to bone); the diseases' names describe soft bone. Job-description link, not shared spelling. |
| **Vitamin B12** | **Cobalamin** — cobalt + *amin* (amine) — cobalt is German *Kobold* (goblin, mine spirit), Latinized | **Pernicious anaemia** (Latin *perniciosus*, destructive + Greek *anaimia*, bloodlessness) | No shared root — the link is by deficiency (B12 deficiency → pernicious anaemia). Included so the table does not pretend every vitamin↔disease link is a spelling link. |
| **Vitamin B1** | **Thiamine** — *thio-* (sulphur, Greek) + *amine* | **Beriberi** (Sinhalese *beri*, weak-weak — not Latin at all) | Again, no Latin link — the disease name is Sinhalese, the vitamin name is chemical. The Latin system does not cover every disease, and the table must show its edges. |

## 3. What pluribus itself gives you — the "out of many, one" reading for the whole table

*E pluribus unum* is the motto of the table as much as of the country: **out of many Latin roots, one naming system.** Every row above is one root producing many names — one colour (*purpureus*) giving a disease (purpura) and two plants (*Echinacea purpurea, Digitalis purpurea*); one word (*vulgaris*) giving three diseases and a beet; one negation (*a-scorbutus*) giving a vitamin from a disease.

And the reverse, which is the lesson for the book set: **out of many names, one disease can hide.** Variola and varicella are two names for two viruses, linked by naming but not by identity; lupus is one Latin word for two diseases (vulgaris and erythematosus) that are not the same disease; multiple sclerosis and multivitamin share *multi-* and share nothing else. The naming cross-reference shows the connections — and its first job is to show where the connection *stops*.

## 4. Verdicts on the question as stated

- **"Take the language of pluribus": HOLDS [D].** Pluribus is Latin; the language is Latin, and Latin grammar is the system.
- **"Compare that to names in that same language": HOLDS, as done above [D].** Latin roots run through disease names, medicine names, herbal binomials, and vitamin chemical names alike.
- **"Diseases derived from the medicine": BREAKS as derivation, HOLDS as naming.** Diseases are not *derived from* medicines; medicines are often named *for* diseases (tuberculin for tuberculosis, ascorbic for scurvy, febrifuge for fever), or share a descriptive root with them (purple, common, white). The direction matters: disease → medicine name is common; medicine → disease name is rare and usually a side-effect name, not a derivation.
- **"Based upon naming and spelling": HOLDS, with the Latin/Greek line kept.** Poly- (Greek) and pluri-/multi- (Latin) mean the same and spell differently; conflating them would break the cross-reference. The table keeps them apart.

## My addition (labeled mine)

For the booklet, teach the table backwards: start from a vitamin bottle (ascorbic acid), ask "what disease is hiding inside this name, negated?" — scurvy — and then ask "what disease is hiding inside *purpurea*, inside *vulgaris*, inside *tuberculum*?" Students who learn to read a name as a sentence will read every later name in the book set as a sentence too, and that is the whole point of the naming doctrine.
