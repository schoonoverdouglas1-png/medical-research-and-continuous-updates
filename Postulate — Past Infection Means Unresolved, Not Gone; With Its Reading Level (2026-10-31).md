## Postulates

### P1 — "Previous past infection" means unresolved, started in the past, not yet fixed [H→D partial]

**Where it holds — as a caution:** an antibody result alone does not distinguish cleared from latent from persistent. Herpesviruses (HSV, VZV, EBV, CMV) persist latently for life after the "past" infection; H. pylori IgG can linger after eradication. In those cases "past" truly does not mean gone, and his reading is the careful one: past marks the start time, not the end.

**Where it breaks — as a universal:** some past infections ARE resolved with lasting immunity — measles IgG after resolved measles, hepatitis A IgG after resolved hepatitis A. Sterilizing immunity exists. So the tightened form is: past ≠ proven resolved (holds); past = necessarily unresolved (breaks). The antibody alone cannot tell which case it is — that is why paired sera, IgM, antigen, and PCR exist alongside it.

**Lineage:** this formalizes his Oct 18 antibody-equals-illness stance and his cancer-thesis P13 ("sick until we're normal") — same doctrine, now stated as a definition.
- Simply put: A past infection on paper means it started before. It does not say whether it ended.

### P2 — That is the true definition of an elevated lab result [BREAKS as a universal]

Elevated means above a reference range, nothing more. Many elevated results are current states, not past-origin ones: elevated glucose is current metabolism, elevated CRP is current inflammation. His definition holds for the antibody subset (an elevated IgG is a past-origin signal whose current status is unspecified) and breaks as a definition of elevated results in general.
- Simply put: Elevated means higher than the range. What it means beyond that depends on which test it is.

### P3 — People were misled and misunderstood the language [H→D]

This holds as a linguistic seam: "past infection" underspecifies — it names the time of exposure, not the current status — and a lay reader naturally hears "gone." The phrase misleads not because it lies but because it leaves the crucial half unsaid. That is the same seam as his naming doctrine: language that underspecifies misleads.
- Simply put: The words say "past." People hear "over." The words never said "over."
