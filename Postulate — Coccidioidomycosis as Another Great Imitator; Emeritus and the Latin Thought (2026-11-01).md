## P2 — "Emeritus" in Latin shares the thought [H, two readings; root leg BREAKS]

**His claim:** "Emeritus," in Latin, is part of the etymology of that, and shares that thought.

**Tested:**
1. *Etymology, straight:* emeritus is Latin, from emereri — e- (out) + mereri (to earn, deserve): one who has served out his time and keeps the title after the office ends. Imitator is Latin too, from imitari (to imitate, copy). The two words do NOT share a root. As a shared-root claim, this BREAKS.
2. *The shared thought, as a rhyme:* an emeritus keeps the title after the substance of the role ends; a great imitator shows the appearance of a disease without being that disease. Title-without-substance is the rhyme — and that rhyme is plausibly exactly what he meant by "shares that thought." Filed as his thought, not as etymology.
3. *Alternative reading:* he may have meant Latin generally — both words are Latin-derived, and under his Latinology doctrine Latin naming itself carries the meaning. If that was the point, the Latin leg HOLDS as a doctrine claim, not a root claim.

**Verdict:** root-sharing BREAKS; thought-sharing HOLDS as his rhyme (title without substance ≈ appearance without disease); Latin-generally reading HOLDS as doctrine. His confirmation of which he meant decides between readings 2 and 3.
- Simply put: Emeritus and imitator don't come from the same Latin root. But they rhyme in meaning: a title kept after the job ends, and a disease's looks kept without the disease.

## Combined statement

If the reading is right, valley fever joins Lyme in the great-imitator set — one fungal, one bacterial, both wearing other diseases' clothes. And the Latin word he reached for, emeritus, does not share imitator's root, but it shares its thought: what remains when the substance is gone — a title, an appearance — and that remainder is what fools the eye.
