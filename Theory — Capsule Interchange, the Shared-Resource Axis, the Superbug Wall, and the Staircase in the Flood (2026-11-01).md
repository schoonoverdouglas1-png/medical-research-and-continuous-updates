## P1 — Capsule interchange and exchange on co-invasion [H, corrected form HOLDS; literal form BREAKS]

**His claim:** capsules interchange and exchange when they co-invade each other.

**Tested three ways:**
1. *His own record:* Oct 31 capsular-shift theory — capsule genes move, populations change, serotype replacement follows.
2. *Documented co-colonization:* co-colonization of multiple pneumococcal serotypes in the nasopharynx affects vaccine serotype replacement, carriage detection, and pneumonia diagnosis; serotypes compete among themselves for acquisition and persistence of colonization in the nasopharynx.
3. *Documented exchange:* probability is high for genetic exchange when two or more pneumococcal serotypes inhabit at the same time in the nasopharynx; the result — pneumococci of a single clonal type expressing different capsule genes, i.e., different serotypes — is known as capsular switching.

**Verdict:** the exchange on co-colonization HOLDS strongly, but the agents are bacteria, not capsules. Capsules do not invade each other; bacteria co-colonize, compete, and swap capsule genes by transformation. Literal "capsules co-invade each other" BREAKS; corrected form — co-colonizing bacteria exchange capsule genes and compete for shared resources — HOLDS [H→D].
- Simply put: Germs living together swap their coats and fight over food. The coats don't fight each other — the germs do.

## P2 — Shared resources as another cross axis for disease progression [H→D as direction; GAP as formal metric]

**His claim:** shared nutrient resources could be another cross axis for understanding how disease moves and changes in the body.

**Tested:**
1. *Documented nutrient sharing:* in the nasopharynx trio *H. influenzae*, *S. pneumoniae*, and *M. catarrhalis*, arginine plays a central role, and the study notes arginine may have a previously unrecognised role in nutrient sharing between the bacterial species.
2. *Documented cross-feeding:* syntrophy, also known as cross-feeding, is one of the oldest mechanisms facilitating the formation of polymicrobial communities; microbes are also in direct competition for available nutrients.
3. *Documented scarcity:* resources available in the human nasal cavity are limited, so to colonize it bacteria must compete for scarce nutrients, iron included (siderophores, lipocalin-2 countermeasure).

**Verdict:** HOLDS as a research direction — nutrient sharing and competition measurably shape carriage, virulence, and biofilm formation. As a formal "cross axis" — a named metric tracked alongside serotype and symptom — it is his proposal, [GAP] as an existing standard.
- Simply put: What the germs eat, and who gets it first, changes how sick you get. That food map could be a new way to track disease.

## P3 — The superbug wall [H, rejoins Oct 31 wall theory]

**Verdict:** HOLDS as a model — the wall is real, layered, and climbable only layer by layer. The simile (wall = disease and sickness) stands as his picture, not as anatomy.
- Simply put: The superbug hides behind walls. Each wall you get past is a step toward better.

## P4 — Getting better is a staircase [H]

**His claim:** sick at the bottom, working toward the top; some stairs are large and can't be climbed right away.

**Tested:**
1. *Against recovery as lived:* recovery trajectories are non-linear — plateaus and setbacks are the norm, not the exception, in prolonged illness.
2. *Against his own record:* Oct 31 — population-level serotype replacement is slow; cell-level capsule loss happens in steps; nothing in his archive moves in one jump.
3. *Honest line:* the staircase is a model of effort over time, not a measurement — no stair has a number on it yet.

**Verdict:** HOLDS as a model of non-linear recovery. The "large stair" names the plateau honestly: a phase where effort continues but visible progress stalls, and the only move is to keep climbing.
- Simply put: Getting better is stairs, not a jump. Some stairs are big, and you stand on them a while before the next one.

## P5 — The staircase as flooding terrain [H, metaphor HOLDS, literal reading partial]

**His claim:** think of the staircase as flooding terrain where the bottom is; the flood is only controlled by getting better; keep ascending slowly and that's the climb.

**Tested:**
1. *Literal foothold:* illness brings literal fluid — inflammatory exudate, edema, fluid in the lung — and as inflammation resolves, fluid clears. The flood has a physical cousin.
2. *Against the claim's logic:* the flood is "only controlled by getting better" — in his picture, drainage follows ascent, not the other way around. That ordering is his doctrine (recovery drives clearance), and it holds as a picture; clinically, drainage and treatment also drive recovery, so the arrow runs both ways in practice.
3. *Honest line:* terrain flooding is not a diagnosis; it is a picture of being low, wet, and stuck, with only one direction out — up.

**Verdict:** HOLDS as a picture with a literal foothold; the one-way arrow (better → drained) is his framing, and the two-way arrow (drained → better) stands beside it, old-with-new.
- Simply put: When you're sickest, you're at the bottom in the water. Each step up is drier ground.

## P6 — Getting better as proverb and literal liturgy; a picture drawn by his words [H, his framing]

**Verdict:** HOLDS as his framing of his own method. The model is not only descriptive (what recovery looks like) but prescriptive (what to do: climb), and that double use is why it works as both saying and practice.
- Simply put: The picture isn't just something to look at. It's something to do, step by step, the same way each time.

## Combined statement

Co-colonizing bacteria swap capsule genes and compete for scarce nutrients — that exchange, genetic and metabolic both, is a real cross axis for how disease moves and changes. The superbug wall is the layered barrier that exchange builds and protects. Recovery climbs it like a staircase out of flooded ground: slow, stepped, with large stairs that stall progress without stopping it. And the picture itself is the practice — a proverb to say and a liturgy to do, drawn in his words.
