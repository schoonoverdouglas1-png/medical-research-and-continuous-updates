## 1. What PCR actually measures vs what antibody testing actually measures

### In plain terms

**Simply put:** PCR asks, "Is the organism's genetic material here, right now, in this tube?" Antibody testing asks, "Has this person's immune system reacted to this organism — recently, or at some point in the past?"

- **PCR** finds nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) from the organism itself, in the specimen tested, during the window when that nucleic acid is present in that specimen. [D]
- **Antibody testing** finds the immune system's response (IgM and IgG antibodies) to the organism. It does not find the organism itself. [D]

That one difference explains almost every break in the parallel table in §2.

### In technical terms

**PCR — nucleic acid presence in a window.**
- PCR amplifies a target nucleic-acid sequence from the specimen. A positive result means target nucleic acid was detectable in that specimen at that time, above the assay's limit of detection (LOD). It does not by itself prove live organism, active disease in a particular organ, or disease causation. [D, with the causation point as general laboratory reasoning — see §3]
- PCR is specimen-specific and time-specific. The same virus can be PCR-positive in one specimen and PCR-negative in another at the same time (for example West Nile virus: whole-blood PCR up to 87% sensitive in acute disease vs serum 26% in the cited Mayo-page data — and even within blood fractions, sensitivity differs). [D]
- PCR windows are short for most acute arboviruses: chikungunya serum PCR is for the acute phase, ideally under 7 days from onset [D]; dengue serum PCR is an acute-phase test, and after more than 1 week of symptoms a patient may be PCR-negative and need serology instead [D]; Florida's acute serum PCR for Eastern equine, St. Louis, and West Nile is under 5 days [D]; Powassan serum PCR is for the first 7 days [D]; Venezuelan equine viraemia may be detectable only up to about 3 days after onset [D]; Florida Trioplex serum is under 14 days [D]. A PCR test taken after its window has closed cannot answer, however good the assay is.

**Antibody testing — immune response over time.**
- **IgM** is generally the earlier antibody class: it rises first after infection and is used as a marker of recent infection, but IgM can persist, cross-react, and (for some viruses) reappear — it is not a clock. [D, general immunology as reflected in the reports' acute/convalescent framing]
- **IgG** rises later and generally persists far longer, sometimes for life. A positive IgG alone usually means past exposure or past infection, not current disease. [D]
- **Acute vs convalescent:** the standard way to show a recent infection serologically is a *paired* sample — an acute serum and a convalescent serum taken at least 2 weeks apart (the Oropouche PRNT pattern in the reports: acute plus convalescent sera at least 2 weeks apart preferred) — looking for a significant rise in titre, not a single positive value. [D]
- **Reference values in the reports illustrate the point:** Mayo ARBOP serum reference is less than 1:10 for IgG and IgM for all four viruses, all ages; CSF reference is less than 1:1. A titre at or above those cut-offs is a *showing*, not a diagnosis, and its meaning depends on timing, specimen, and confirmatory testing. [D]

**Cross-reactivity and the 4-fold PRNT rule — where the reports mention it.**
- Closely related viruses cross-react serologically. The ARBO report names dengue vs St. Louis antigens, and Eastern vs Western equine, as cross-reactivity pairs, and notes a typically 8-fold or greater difference for the infecting virus in the Eastern/Western equine comparison. [D — that 8-fold figure is the Eastern/Western equine context only; do not apply it to every pair]
- For the California serogroup, the DR/Mexico report states the laboratory standard differently: a 4-fold or greater higher neutralising titre is required to identify the specific virus, because La Crosse is the actual assay target in the Focus IFA and a "California" result may reflect another serogroup member (Jamestown Canyon, snowshoe hare, Tahyna, Inkoo, Trivittatus, Guaroa, and others share the serogroup). [D]
- **PRNT (plaque-reduction neutralisation test) is the confirmatory method for these cross-reactivity problems, and it is largely public-health-only:** the reports note neutralisation tests that help differentiate arboviral pathogens are only available through public-health laboratories (Philadelphia guidance, cited in the DR/Mexico report), and that CDC/PRNT routes apply for Oropouche, Mayaro, Venezuelan equine confirmation, and flavivirus confirmation generally. [D]
- **Simply put:** antibodies are blurry photographs of related viruses. PRNT is the sharper photograph, and it mostly lives in public-health laboratories, not in commercial panels.

**A note on "ARBO" itself, because the whole parallel depends on it:** there is no Mayo test ID "ARBO". [D] The closest Mayo IDs are ARBOP (serum arbovirus *antibody* panel, IFA — not PCR) and ABOPC (CSF arbovirus antibody panel, IFA). "ARBO" and "ARBS" are local client/EHR codes that map to ARBOP. Any record that says "ARBO panel" must be checked for whether it means ARBOP (serum antibody), ABOPC (CSF antibody), or a non-Mayo local panel — it does not denote a Mayo PCR panel. [D]

## 3. Timing as the load-bearing difference

**Simply put:** the test you need depends on the day you are on. Too early, the antibody hasn't arrived yet. Too late, the virus has already left the blood. Wrong specimen, the answer was never in that tube at all.

### Why a negative blood PCR does not exclude central nervous system disease [D]
- Many meningitis/encephalitis pathogens cause brain and meningeal disease without sustained viraemia or bacteraemia at the time of testing. The textbook case in the reports is HSV encephalitis: CSF PCR is the diagnostic method of choice, and serum HSV antibody is not of utility for diagnosing HSV encephalitis in adults. By extension, a negative *blood* PCR for a CNS pathogen is even weaker evidence against CNS infection — the pathogen may simply not be in blood. [D]
- The PCR window may already have closed: dengue after 1 week, chikungunya after 7 days, Eastern equine/St. Louis/West Nile acute serum under 5 days in the Florida menu, Powassan in the first 7 days, Venezuelan equine viraemia up to about 3 days. A negative PCR after those windows is a statement about timing, not about absence of disease. [D]
- Matrix alone can hide the target: blood inhibitors (heme/haemoglobin, immunoglobulins, heparin, bilirubin, lipids) inhibit polymerase, and the same pathogen can have a different limit of detection in blood vs CSF (HSV blood LOD higher than CSF LOD in the listing cited). Diluting to reduce inhibitors also dilutes target below the LOD — a trade-off that must be re-validated per matrix. [D]
- **Simply put:** "not in the blood today" is not the same sentence as "not in the brain."

### Why a positive antibody does not prove current disease [D]
- IgG persists after past infection, sometimes for life. A single positive IgG, without a paired acute/convalescent rise, a compatible timing, and a clinical picture, proves exposure, not current disease. [D, from §1]
- Cross-reactivity makes a single positive even blurrier: a dengue result can cross-react with St. Louis antigen, Eastern equine with Western equine, and any California-serogroup result can reflect another serogroup member. That is why the reports require a 4-fold or greater neutralising titre to speciate within the California serogroup, and why PRNT confirmation lives mostly in public-health laboratories. [D]
- **Simply put:** an antibody is a footprint. It tells you something walked there — not that it is still standing there.

### Why latent virus in blood does not prove brain disease [D]
- HHV-6 integrates into host DNA and is detectable in blood lymphocytes in a large fraction of healthy people. A positive HHV-6 blood PCR therefore does not prove HHV-6 brain disease, just as a positive CSFME HHV-6 result (sensitivity 85.7% in CSF in the evaluation cited, with high false-positivity on discrepancy analysis) does not by itself prove it. The specimen, the quantification, the clinical picture, and the exclusion of other causes all have to be weighed — and that weighing belongs to clinicians, not to this analysis. [D]
- **Simply put:** finding a familiar tenant in the blood does not prove that tenant broke into the brain.

### Why timing (days since onset) decides which test can even answer [D]
- **Days 0–3:** acute serum PCR is at its best for Venezuelan equine (up to about 3 days), Eastern equine/St. Louis/West Nile acute serum (under 5 days in the Florida menu), and chikungunya/Powassan (under 7 days). Antibody may not yet be detectable.
- **Days 0–7:** chikungunya and Powassan serum PCR windows; dengue serum PCR still acute, but approaching its edge.
- **After 7 days:** dengue, chikungunya, and similar acute PCRs lose sensitivity; serology becomes the route (dengue IgG/IgM, chikungunya IgM/IgG, Zika IgM, West Nile IgG/IgM). Heartland serology via CDC is specifically the fallback when symptoms exceed 1 week and PCR is negative. [D]
- **After 14 days:** Florida Trioplex serum window has closed (under 14 days); paired acute/convalescent serology (at least 2 weeks apart) becomes the confirmatory pattern, as in Oropouche PRNT. [D]
- **Simply put:** PCR is a photograph of the first days. Antibody is a diary of the weeks after. Asking the photograph question in week three, or the diary question on day one, gives you the wrong kind of answer.

## 4. Anything else useful in that direction — only where the reports support it

### Antigen testing: dengue NS1 [D]
- Dengue NS1 antigen is a *third* method, neither PCR nor antibody: it detects a viral protein in serum during the acute phase. Mayo DENVP is a dengue antibody/antigen panel (IgG + IgM + NS1 antigen), serum 1 mL; DNSAG is dengue NS1 antigen alone, serum. The commercial VIDAS Dengue Panel (NS1 antigen + anti-dengue IgM + IgG) is a *kit* used by local laboratories abroad, not a Mayo test, and must not be cited as one. [D]
- **Simply put:** PCR finds the virus's instructions, antigen finds one of its products, antibody finds the body's reply. Three different witnesses, three different windows.

### Culture [D]
- Mayo's meningitis algorithm includes **blood cultures** (culture, not PCR) alongside lumbar puncture, CSFME, CSF cell count/protein/glucose, Gram stain, and bacterial culture. Culture answers "does a live bacterium grow from this specimen?" — a different question from PCR's "is bacterial nucleic acid present?", slower, and specimen-dependent. It is part of the meningitis workup in the algorithm, not a substitute for PCR, and not a blood PCR panel. [D]
- No routine viral culture panel for meningitis/encephalitis is presented as a commercial alternative in the reports; viral culture appears only as a research/case finding (for example Madariaga plasma virus culture in a Haitian case), not as an orderable panel. [D]

### Metagenomic sequencing — not PCR, not a panel in the PCR sense [D]
- **MSCSF** (spinal fluid only, 2 mL, lumbar-puncture CSF only, shunt/ventricular fluid not acceptable, NY: Yes) is metagenomic next-generation sequencing of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites in CSF. It is not PCR, it is not blood, and it requires a spinal tap — parallel only for Douglas, never an option. [D]
- **Karius Spectrum (KPLA/KDPLA, plasma)** is plasma microbial cell-free DNA metagenomic sequencing covering more than 1,000 bacteria, fungi, DNA viruses, and parasites, **performed by Karius Laboratory** and ordered through Mayo. It is not a Mayo PCR panel, not encephalitis/meningitis-specific, and its breadth is not the same as a targeted panel's sensitivity for any one organism. [D]
- **Simply put:** sequencing reads everything in the tube; PCR asks about one thing (or a fixed list) very precisely. Breadth and precision are a trade-off, not the same virtue.

### Specimen-type validation — why a CSF assay cannot simply be run on blood [D]
This is the load-bearing "anything else" in the reports, because it is the reason the blood parallel has gaps at all:
- **Intended use is specimen-specific.** BioFire FilmArray ME is intended for CSF specimens obtained via lumbar puncture; QIAstat-Dx ME (US) is intended for CSF specimens obtained via lumbar puncture. Neither syndromic panel is validated for blood. [D]
- **Changing specimen type changes the test's regulatory status.** Under CLIA (42 CFR 493.1253), an unmodified FDA-cleared test needs *verification* (accuracy, precision, reportable range, reference interval). A *modified* test — and a different specimen type is explicitly listed as a modification — or a laboratory-developed test needs *full establishment* of performance specifications before patient reporting: accuracy, precision, analytical sensitivity, analytical specificity **including interfering substances**, reportable range, reference interval, and matrix validation, with laboratory-director sign-off. FDA's 2024 laboratory-developed-test-as-device rule was vacated by a federal court in March 2025 and reverted in September 2025, leaving CLIA as the primary framework in the reports. [D]
- **What laboratories actually do instead of swapping specimens:** they validate the same pathogen separately for each specimen. Mayo is the clearest example — chikungunya has CHIKS (serum) *and* CHIKC (CSF) as separate test IDs; dengue has DENGS (serum) *and* DENGC (CSF); West Nile and Powassan each have serum, CSF, and urine forms as separate IDs with separate limits of detection. If swapping were accepted, separate IDs, volumes, and LODs would be unnecessary. [D]
- **The documented repurposing loss (opposite direction, but the best analogue in the reports):** repurposing the BioFire Blood Culture Identification 2 panel (a blood-culture panel) for CSF gave about 90% sensitivity when organisms were seen on Gram stain, but only about 50% when Gram stain was negative, versus standard-of-care testing. The authors present it as a study of utility, not as a validated clinical offering. [D]
- **No laboratory was found offering the BioFire or QIAstat meningitis/encephalitis panel on blood as a validated clinical test, and no sensitivity or specificity for "ME-on-blood" exists to quote.** Any claim that a specific laboratory *could* validate it successfully, what its sensitivity *would* be, or that a future multi-matrix cartridge *will* appear, is speculation in the reports and is flagged as such here. Vendor marketing about "one cartridge handles CSF, blood, or swab" is not evidence of a validated blood panel. [D]
- **Simply put:** PCR chemistry does not know what tube the sample came from — but the *claims* about the test do. A signal from an unvalidated specimen is a signal without a validated meaning.

### Same test, different name vs different test, same name [D]
- **Same Mayo test, different local code:** UW Medicine Lab 646, Children's Minnesota ARBS, CentraCare LAB0298463, WakeMed LAB3972, and HealthLab 2192910 all resolve to Mayo ARBOP/ABOPC — same 4 viruses, same IFA method, Rochester performing laboratory. The check is fourfold: performing laboratory = Mayo Clinic Laboratories in Rochester, Mayo test ID ARBOP/ABOPC, method IFA, and the 4-virus list. If any of those differs (PCR instead of IFA, dengue/Zika/Ross River in the list, a public-health laboratory as performer), it is a different test even if it is called an "arbovirus panel." [D]
- **Different test, same generic name:** Canterbury (New Zealand) Arbovirus PCR (only chikungunya, dengue, Zika, by PCR); PathWest (Australia) Arbovirus Serology (history-selected, including Japanese encephalitis, Murray Valley, Kunjin/West Nile, yellow fever, chikungunya, Ross River, Barmah Forest); Wadsworth Travel Panel 6150 (dengue, chikungunya, Oropouche, Zika, by PCR); Minnesota public-health ARBO (may include West Nile, Powassan, Jamestown Canyon, Eastern/Western equine, California-group, St. Louis — broader than Mayo ARBOP, and including the Jamestown Canyon analyte Mayo lacks); UK geographic panels chosen by region, symptoms, and travel, not a fixed panel. [D]
- **Jacksonville duplicates are not different tests:** LHSV/LHSVB (= HSVPV/HSVPB), LCMV (= CMVPV), LVZV (= VZVPV), LHSVZ (= HSVPV + VZVPV) mean the specimen went to Jacksonville, Florida, not that the assay or the country differs. [D]
- **New York is a state restriction, not a country difference:** CSFME = No and ABOPC = not available for New York-origin specimens, while ARBOP, WNVS, TIKLB, ENS2, MSCSF, POWVC, HRTVS, FLARP, and QHV6P = Yes. No other US state panel ban was found in the pages reviewed, and no Mayo country ban or country-specific panel was found. [D]
