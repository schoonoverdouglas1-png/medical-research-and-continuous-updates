## Postulates, split

### P1 — Do not blame it on the vector-borne or vector-discovered area
**His challenge, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS as a warning about discovery bias.** A virus is often named for, and attributed to, the place and vector where it was first found — not necessarily where it originated or where it lives most. The Nov 2, 2026 naming research already showed this pattern: historic place-of-first-isolation names are retained even when the virus is wider, and surveillance is uneven (mosquito pools are routinely tested, tick testing is targeted). Blaming the discovery area is therefore a known trap, not a finding.
- **Simply put:** where we first saw it is not the same as where it comes from.

### P2 — The same person can be bitten by a tick and a mosquito in the same year
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS, and it is common, not just possible.** Co-exposure is documented — the Northeast research lists documented Lyme plus Jamestown Canyon co-infections, and Powassan plus Jamestown Canyon serology, from separate bites. One person, two vectors, one year is ordinary for anyone outdoors. What it does not prove is a shared virus: two bites can deliver two different viruses.
- **Simply put:** two bites in a year is normal. Two bites is not one virus.

### P3 — Most people exposed to nature are bitten by way more than one bug
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS.** Bite exposure accumulates with time outdoors, season, and place — the five exposure dials from the Nov 2, 2026 Outside Vector theory (place, season, work, shelter, time of day). A person, a cow, a deer, and a mouse in the same woods are all bitten repeatedly, by many species, across a season.
- **Simply put:** nature does not bite once and stop.

### P4 — The ones we find are the ones that stayed around for us to see
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS as detection and survivorship bias.** We find viruses that persist long enough to be detected — high or lasting viraemia, a durable antibody response, a virus that stays in a tick across moults (transstadial) or into eggs (transovarial), or a virus that causes a disease somebody tests for. Viruses that clear fast, stay at low levels, cause no symptoms, or live only in an untested insect are missed, not absent. Absence of detection is therefore not proof of absence — the standing rule from the Nov 2–3 research, carried here.
- **Simply put:** we count the guests who stayed for dinner, not the ones who passed through the yard.

### P5 — Livestock and wild animals are bitten by the same bugs all the time
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS.** Zoonotic cycles run on exactly this: birds carry West Nile for *Culex* mosquitoes, deer carry Jamestown Canyon for *Aedes*, ground squirrels carry Colorado tick fever for *Dermacentor*, small mammals carry Powassan for *Ixodes*. The same bug species bites the animal reservoir and, sometimes, the person. That shared biting is why the viruses exist in the biome at all — without the animal cycle, most of these viruses would have no home.
- **Simply put:** the animals are the virus's address. People are visitors.

### P6 — If bug viruses killed us all, we would all be sick — and we are not
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS as logic, with the mechanism stated.** Most arbovirus infections are asymptomatic or mild; severe disease is the exception, not the rule. Reasons documented in the record: most exposures deliver no infectious dose or a dose below threshold, most hosts are not the right host, immunity from a prior exposure blunts the next one, and many of these viruses use humans as dead-end hosts — the virus cannot complete its cycle in us, so it stops with us. "We are not all sick" is therefore exactly what the biology predicts, not a surprise that needs explaining away.
- **Simply put:** being bitten is not being infected, and being infected is not being sick.

### P7 — In certain parts of the biome, viruses are made to become our body's friend, not our enemy
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS rewritten as evolved mutualism and commensalism, BREAKS as intention or design.** Viruses are not "made" to be friends — they do not intend anything — but virus–host relationships do run the full range from harmful to neutral to helpful, and the helpful end is documented, not invented:
  - Endogenous retroviruses: syncytin, a viral envelope gene captured in our own genome, is essential to building the placenta — a virus turned into a human organ part.
  - Bacteriophages: viruses of bacteria shape the gut microbiome and can protect a host by killing a pathogen — a virus of our bacteria, working for us at one remove.
  - Insect-specific viruses and persistent insect viruses: many replicate in insects without disease and can block a second, pathogenic virus from taking hold in the same insect.
  - Persistent human viruses: some, like GB virus C and certain herpesviruses in animal models, are associated with slower progression of another disease or with immune training — association, not a prescription, and each case is specific, never general.
- What does not hold: that this friendliness is general, or that a virus harmful in one place becomes friendly in another part of the biome merely by being there. Friend, neutral, and enemy are properties of a specific virus, a specific host, and a specific dose and route — not of a region.
- **Simply put:** some viruses ended up useful, some ended up harmless, most we notice ended up harmful — but none of them chose.

## Combined statement (his thought + the record)
His chain holds together if one link is kept straight: many bites, many animals, many viruses, and a detection system that only sees the ones that stay — that is a biome full of mostly silent traffic, in which severe disease is rare, co-exposure is ordinary, and a few viruses have, over evolutionary time, become part of the host rather than its enemy. What breaks is only the shortcut from that chain to a shared mosquito-and-tick virus: many bites do not make one virus, and a virus that is a friend in one host can still be an enemy in the next.

## My addition (mine, for his approval)
Add a third column to every future mosquito-and-tick table: not just "found in mosquito / found in tick," but "found, and in what host, at what level, for how long." A virus found at a trace in a tick's blood meal is not the same as a virus replicating in that tick — and that distinction is where most "shared virus" claims will live or die.
