"Biome byproduct" — kept as his term; the standard parallels are dysbiosis product / microbial metabolite / shed community, kept separate in P5.

## His dictation, verbatim

> "When it comes to the theory of the viruses the thought process is that percentage of tests would have to dictate that if the testing almost always gives that parallel when tested that there's a virus there but if you don't test the assumption would almost have to be from my scientific perspective that viruses are always present whenever the fungus is present and when you clear disease out of your body the viruses and the fungus are no longer there and fungus is always a byproduct of the biome and when you're sick there will be an overburden and that's when the when the biome changes because of the sickness of the bugs and us both is why we have biome byproduct that is what illness actually is.."

## Split postulates

### P1. The percentage of tests dictates the assumption [H]
**Test:**
1. **Base-rate logic [D]:** a percentage *does* license a probability — if 90 of 100 tested fungi carry a virus, the next tested fungus has roughly a 90% prior chance. That is legitimate Bayesian reasoning, and his instinct to use the percentage is sound.
2. **The condition that must travel with the percentage [D]:** the percentage licenses a probability *for the same kind of case that was tested*. A rate from sick, cultured, or clinically selected fungi does not transfer to all fungi everywhere. This is selection bias, the oldest trap in test-percentage reasoning.
3. **The number on file [D]:** the general fungal survey found 21.6% positive for RNA mycoviruses in early-diverging fungi, with other fungi ranging 6.6% to 97% by species and screen. If his tested set runs near 100%, his set is a *selected* set — diseased, cultured, or otherwise filtered — and the file must say which, or the percentage misleads.
**Verdict: HOLDS as conditional probability; BREAKS as an unconditional license.**
**Simply put:** If almost every fungus you test has a virus, it's fair to bet the next similar one will too. It's not fair to bet that about every fungus in the world — your tested batch may be special.

### P2. If testing almost always gives the parallel, a virus is there [H]
**Test:** this is P1 applied to one case. It holds *as a bet* (high prior), not as a finding. A bet is not a test result, and the book must label it as such: "assumed present, untested" is a different tier from "detected."
**Verdict: HOLDS as a high-probability assumption, BREAKS as a detection claim.**
**Simply put:** "Almost always" means you can reasonably assume it — but assuming isn't the same as finding it. Keep those two words apart.

### P3. If you don't test, assume viruses are always present whenever fungus is present [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **"Always" vs. the survey [D]:** 21.6% in one broad survey, 30–80% estimated across species — neither is "always." The assumption overstates even the high end.
2. **The untested case [LOGIC]:** an untested fungus has no virus measurement at all. Assuming presence fills a GAP with a certainty the record does not support. His own standing rule says GAP stays GAP where the record is empty at 51% — and "always" claims 100%.
3. **The conditional save [H→D]:** restated conditionally, it holds: "in the kind of diseased fungus I test, assume a virus until a test says otherwise, and test when it matters." That version is testable, falsifiable, and honest — and it is the version the book should carry, with his original "always" preserved beside it, old-with-new.
**Verdict: BREAKS as "always"; HOLDS as a conditional working assumption for his tested kind of case.**
**Simply put:** Don't write "always" where you mean "in the sick fungi I actually test, almost always." The second sentence is strong and true. The first overreaches and breaks on the numbers.

### P4. When you clear disease out of your body, the viruses and the fungus are no longer there [H]
**Test:**
1. **Acute clearance [D]:** for an acute pathogenic fungus or virus, recovery does track falling load and, often, clearance below detection. That leg holds.
2. **Commensal fungi stay [D]:** "Candida albicans, often associated with infections, was detected in 63.6% of healthy participants", and "As a gut commensal, Candida albicans is capable of adapting to the intestinal environment without causing harm under normal circumstances". Healthy people carry fungi after recovery — and before sickness.
3. **Commensal viruses stay [D]:** anelloviruses are "acquired in infancy and maintain a lifelong, dynamic presence", with prevalence in healthy adults "approach[ing] 100%". They do not leave when disease clears, because they were never the disease.
4. **Endogenous viruses never leave [D]:** HERV sequences are in the genome itself; no clearance removes them.
**Verdict: HOLDS for the acute disease pair (pathogenic fungus + its virus falling below detection); BREAKS universal — commensal fungi, commensal viruses, and endogenous sequences remain after recovery.**
**Simply put:** When you beat a bad infection, that bad fungus and that bad virus do go away. But the good fungi in your gut and the quiet viruses you've carried since infancy stay — they were never the disease.

### P5. Fungus is always a byproduct of the biome [H]
**Test:**
1. **Fungus as member [D]:** "Fungi are commensal members of the host; they are prevalent in various body sites, such as the gut, lungs, and skin". The mycobiome is a *part* of the biome, not merely a product of it.
2. **Fungus as producer [D]:** fungi *make* byproducts — metabolites, cell-wall components, toxins in some species — that then affect bacteria, host immunity, and other fungi. Member and producer are both true; "byproduct only" drops the member half.
3. **"Always" again [LOGIC]:** a fungus that colonizes from the environment (dietary *Saccharomyces*, inhaled spores) is not a byproduct of *your* biome at all — it is an input to it. Byproduct language fits shed material and metabolites; it does not fit colonizers.
**Verdict: HOLDS partial — fungi produce biome byproducts and shed as byproducts; BREAKS as "fungus is always (only) a byproduct."**
**Simply put:** Fungi are residents of your biome, not just its trash. They do make byproducts — but they also live there, eat there, and change things there.

### P6. When you're sick there will be an overburden, and the biome changes because of the sickness of the bugs and us both [H]
**Test:**
1. **Overburden in sickness [D]:** dysbiosis — reduced diversity, expansion of one group — is documented in sickness (IBD, obesity, COPD patterns filed the same hour). Overburden as a skewed, expanded load holds.
2. **Two-way sickness [H→D]:** "the sickness of the bugs and us both" is the strongest sentence in this dictation. It captures a documented loop: host inflammation changes the microbial environment (oxygen, mucus, bile, antibiotics), which changes the microbes, which changes host inflammation further. Neither side is the sole cause; the loop is the disease process.
3. **The limit [D]:** not every sickness shows a biome overburden first — trauma, genetic disease, and single-pathogen infections with a normal background biome are counterexamples. Overburden is a common pattern, not a universal gate.
**Verdict: HOLDS as a two-way loop in biome-linked sickness; BREAKS as a universal "whenever sick, overburden."**
**Simply put:** When the biome-linked kind of sickness hits, it's not just you that's sick or just the bugs — you're both sick together, and each makes the other worse. That's a loop, not a one-way street.

### P7. Biome byproduct is what illness actually is [H, his definition]
**Test:**
1. **As a definition for biome-linked illness [H→D]:** if illness is defined, in his system, as the changed biome's output — altered metabolites, shed organisms, inflammatory signals — then the definition is coherent and testable: measure the byproduct, name the illness.
2. **Against his six tracks [LOGIC]:** his standing system keeps disease, illness, and sickness as separate tracks. Defining illness *as* biome byproduct does not collapse them *if* disease stays as the named condition and sickness stays as the felt state, with illness as the biome's changed output between them. The book must show that three-part mapping explicitly, or this definition will read as a collapse.
3. **As a universal definition [D]:** breaks — a broken bone's illness, a genetic illness, a purely toxic illness need no biome byproduct to be illness. The definition holds for the biome-linked class, not for all illness.
**Verdict: HOLDS as a definition for biome-linked illness, with the three-track mapping shown; BREAKS as a definition of all illness.**
**Simply put:** For the sicknesses that come from your gut and lung communities going wrong, "illness is what that changed community puts out" is a good, sharp definition. For every illness in the world, it's too narrow — some illnesses don't come from the biome at all.

## My labeled addition (mine, not his)

**Write the percentage on the assumption, every time.** His rule becomes usable — and safe — in one sentence: "In [this kind of fungus, tested this way], [X]% carried a virus; in untested cases of the same kind, I assume a virus at that percentage, labeled as an assumption, not a finding." That sentence honors his scientific instinct (use the rate), obeys his GAP rule (don't claim 100% from 90%), and gives the book a repeatable method. Every "always" in this dictation should be rewritten into that sentence in the book's Chapter 10, with the original "always" preserved beside it.
