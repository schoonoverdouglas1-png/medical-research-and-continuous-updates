# Proposal — Government Lab Standards, One Physician Standard, and AI Reading the Notes (2026-10-31)

**Belief stated:** Douglas, Oct 31, 2026, ~2:41 PM CDT — "I believe that my gov need to set laboratories to testing standards and they have to come up with a way to actually fix everybody and force a standard that all physicians practice because the good ones already do and the bad ones practice a bad standard and I know AI can see it both ways and there's only really a good doctor and a bad doctor and I know with clinical notes and documentation through the research and development that Epic has done alone most likely on the subject and with AI personality is easily seen probably for somebody like me I can read through notes and see it easy but for AI it can probably do that in seconds that would take me years to do and read and write."

**Term kept:** "my gov" is kept as his words — which government he means (United States, tribal, or both) is his to specify. The proposal fits his standing pro-government stance (his book theme: pro-government, pro-insurance, pro-Medicare, pro-CMS).

## Postulates

### P1 — Government must set laboratories to testing standards [H→D partial: the lab half already exists]

**What already exists:** the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988 (CLIA) apply to all U.S. facilities or sites that test human specimens for health or disease assessment, supported by CDC in partnership with CMS and FDA, to ensure laboratories meet standards for accurate, reliable, and timely testing. The lab-quality half of his proposal is already federal law.

**What does not yet exist:** his fuller version — a single standard not just for lab quality but for interpretation and physician practice. No single federally forced practice standard exists; guidelines come from specialty societies, enforcement runs through state boards and malpractice, fragmented. That gap is the new part of his proposal, not the lab part.
- Simply put: The government already sets the lab standard. Your proposal extends it to the reading of the result and the practice around it.

### P2 — Force one standard; good doctors already follow it, bad ones practice a bad standard [H, his proposal; binary breaks, variation holds]

**The binary breaks:** competence runs on a spectrum, and a "bad standard" often reflects the system around the doctor — time pressure, documentation burden, incentives — not only the individual. Good doctors err; struggling doctors improve. Two boxes cannot hold the profession.

**The variation holds:** wide variation in practice is well documented — clinicians treating the same condition differently without outcome differences. That variation is the factual core of his binary: some practice follows the evidence standard, some does not. The proposal's force is aimed at that variation.
- Simply put: Not two kinds of doctor — one standard, unevenly followed.

### P3 — AI can see it both ways, from clinical notes [H→D partial]

**What holds:** Epic's Cosmos research database — a de-identified database now exceeding 300 million patient records from over 38,000 clinics and 1,600 hospitals — already powers case-comparison tools: a "lookalikes" feature that checks whether a patient's case is unusual against similar cases was used 25,000 times in the past year. Epic (Verona, Wisconsin) builds AI charting, assistants, and predictive models on that data. Reading millions of notes for patterns is exactly what this machinery does.

**The seam — "personality":** what AI sees in notes is documentation patterns — ordering choices, guideline concordance, note style, follow-through — not personality itself, and not the whole encounter. Notes omit examination nuance, conversation, and constraints. So: practice patterns visible at scale HOLDS; personality read from notes alone BREAKS as a complete read. Notes are proxies, and a proxy audit needs clinical validation before it judges anyone.
- Simply put: AI can read the pattern in the notes. The pattern is not the whole doctor.

### P4 — AI in seconds versus his years [H→D, split by task]

**Reading volume — holds:** what takes a human years to read (millions of notes), AI reads in seconds to minutes. That half is straightforwardly true at Cosmos scale.

**Judging and writing — the seam:** his "years" include judgment — the reading-through that decides what the pattern means. AI finds the pattern in seconds; deciding whether the pattern is good or bad practice still takes the human standard his proposal would set. And his own skill is the specification: the way he reads notes and sees it easily is the template for what the AI should be taught to look for. He is not replaced by the audit; he is the standard it audits against.

### P5 — "Fix everybody" [BREAKS as a guarantee, HOLDS as a direction]

No standard, government or otherwise, fixes everybody — outcomes cannot be forced, and some conditions cannot be fixed at all (see the good-doctor addendum). What a forced standard plus audit can do is raise the average: fewer missed patterns, fewer dismissed flags, less variation. Direction holds; guarantee does not.
- Simply put: A standard cannot fix everybody. It can make the bad standard harder to hide.

## Combined statement

The lab half of his proposal already exists as CLIA; the new half is extending a single standard from lab quality to interpretation and practice, enforced rather than suggested. The binary of good and bad doctors breaks as a taxonomy but names a real variation; AI can read that variation from notes at a scale no human can match, seeing patterns rather than personalities; and his own note-reading skill is the standard the audit should be taught. The proposal cannot fix everybody, but it can make the bad standard visible — and visibility is where enforcement starts.
