## Split postulates

## Tests

### P1 — Tuberculosis considered pneumonia, names jiving

**HOLDS in a narrow, documented overlap; BREAKS as a general historical statement.**

The documented history, in order:
- TB's old names were **phthisis** and **consumption**, not pneumonia: "Tuberculosis, first called 'phthisis' or commonly, 'consumption', had been around since ancient times" [D, from the history search], and "tuberculosis of the lungs (aka 'consumption' or 'phthisis') was one of the two leading causes of death in the early 1900s (the other was pneumonia)" — TB and pneumonia listed as *two* leading causes, side by side, not as one [D].
- TB was **not identified as a single disease until the 1820s**, and J. L. Schönlein coined the name "tuberculosis" in 1839 [D]. Before that unity, its many forms (lung, glands, bones, joints, abdomen) were spoken of as separate conditions — which is the opposite of "names jived" in the modern sense: the names did *not* yet agree that they were one disease.
- **The foothold for his claim — caseous pneumonia:** Virchow separated "caseous pneumonia" from processes characterized by tubercle formation, starting a "theory of duality" (two diseases, not one), which Koch's 1882 discovery of the tubercle bacillus brought to an end [D]. And Koch's own case list included "twelve cases of caseous pneumonia" in which the bacillus was present [D]. So a *form* of tuberculosis genuinely was called caseous pneumonia, and that form sat exactly on the TB/pneumonia boundary — pulmonary TB with caseous (cheese-like) lung consolidation looks, clinically and on examination, like pneumonia. That is a real, documented overlap, and it is the strongest support for P1.
- Laennec, before Koch, had already "proclaimed the unity of the disease" by separating gangrene, cancer, and other lung processes out from tubercular processes [D] — so the move toward one disease, one name, began before the bacillus, not with it.

So P1, sharpened: *a tubercular lung form was called caseous pneumonia, and pulmonary TB and pneumonia were clinically close enough to be confused — but TB as a whole was historically called phthisis/consumption, and was counted separately from pneumonia as a cause of death.* The overlap is real; the identity ("TB was considered pneumonia") is not.

### P2 — Naming Mycobacterium tuberculosis as one bacterium instead of a group of diseases as one name

**HOLDS in reverse of the direction stated — which is the interesting correction.**

What actually happened, in documented steps:
1. **Group of diseases → one disease name (before the germ):** Schönlein's "tuberculosis" (1839) and Laennec's unity already gathered many forms — pulmonary, glandular, bone, joint, abdominal — under one disease name, because they shared the tubercle as their anatomical basis [D].
2. **One disease → one germ (1882):** Koch identified and described the bacillus causing tuberculosis, *M. tuberculosis*, on 24 March 1882 [D]. That did not replace a group of diseases with one bacterium; it gave the already-unified disease its cause.
3. **One germ name → a group again (modern):** the *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex (MTBC) is "a genetically related group of Mycobacterium species that can cause tuberculosis," including *M. tuberculosis, M. africanum, M. bovis, M. microti, M. canettii, M. caprae,* and others [D]. And since 2018, "all members of this species complex are considered synonyms of *M. tuberculosis* as far as bacterial nomenclature is concerned," at 99.21–99.92% whole-genome identity [D].

So the naming history runs: many disease forms → one disease name → one germ name → a complex (group) of closely related germs → back to one nomenclatural name for the whole complex. His P2 as stated ("one bacterium *instead of* a group of diseases as one name") compresses that loop into a single decision, and the compression reverses the modern step: modern nomenclature does *not* insist on one bacterium instead of a group — it explicitly keeps the group (the complex) and treats its members as synonyms of one name. Both the group and the one name coexist in current usage, which is closer to his "group of diseases as one name" than to a pure single-bacterium naming.

## Combined statement (his thought + tested thought)

His instinct points at a real historical seam: caseous pneumonia, where TB and pneumonia genuinely overlapped in name and in lung, and where Virchow's duality (two diseases) was ended by Koch's bacillus (one cause). The tested correction reorders his story: TB was not generally called pneumonia — it was called phthisis/consumption, counted separately from pneumonia, and only its caseous lung form carried the pneumonia name. And the naming did not move simply from group to one bacterium — it moved from many forms to one disease name, then to one germ, then to a *complex* of germs that modern nomenclature again gathers under one name. The group and the one name were never fully separated; they have been folding into each other for two centuries, and that folding — not a single decision — is what happened.

## My addition (labeled mine)

For the booklet, teach this as a naming timeline, not a naming decision: phthisis/consumption → tuberculosis (1839, one disease name) → tubercle bacillus (1882, one germ) → tuberculosis complex (modern, a group under one name). Students who see the timeline will understand why TB and pneumonia still get confused at the bedside — they share a lung, a cough, and, in caseous pneumonia, a name — without needing TB to *have been* pneumonia.
