- What it is: Streptococcus agalactiae, commonly known as Group B Streptococcus (GBS), is species of bacteria often found naturally in gastrointestinal and genital tracts of healthy people, usually causing no symptoms.
- Why it matters: GBS is major cause of severe infections in newborns, including sepsis (bloodstream infection), pneumonia, and meningitis. It can also cause infections in adults (especially older adults and people with certain medical conditions): urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, bloodstream infections, and less commonly pneumonia or meningitis.
- Pregnancy and newborn risk: About 10% to 30% of pregnant women carry GBS. Screening during pregnancy and preventive antibiotics during labor can reduce risk of early-onset disease in newborns. GBS "colonizes the gastrointestinal and genital tracts of 20%-40% of healthy adults, typically without causing symptoms," and "infections in adults are usually associated with underlying conditions such as diabetes, liver disease, and malignancy."
- Diagnosis: usually identified through bacterial culture or molecular laboratory tests from relevant clinical samples.
- Treatment: GBS infections are typically treated with antibiotics, commonly penicillin-based therapies when appropriate.
- Recent News: In April 2026, World Health Organization (WHO) published updated fact sheet highlighting global burden of GBS disease and noting maternal vaccines are currently in development. Multiple GBS vaccine candidates are advancing through clinical development; July 2026 report noted two vaccine candidates are in late-stage clinical trials, with global health organizations preparing for potential future maternal vaccination programs. Large Phase 3 study evaluating maternal GBS vaccine in pregnant women and their infants is currently underway.
- Key takeaway: "(GBS) is commonly carried without causing illness, but it is an important pathogen because it can cause serious disease in newborns and in adults with certain medical conditions." Copilot also notes: "Multiple multivalent maternal GBS conjugate vaccines are in clinical development, but no GBS vaccine is yet licensed for routine use," and that it may be possible to have one-shot neonatal vaccine (GBS).
