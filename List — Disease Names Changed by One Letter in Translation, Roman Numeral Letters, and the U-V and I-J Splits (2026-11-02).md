# List — Disease Names Changed by One Letter in Translation, Roman Numeral Letters, and the U–V and I–J Splits (2026-11-02)

*Manuscript reference work only — NEVER medical advice. A naming list, tested against the record, not a list of treatments.*

## His question, verbatim (Nov 2, 2026, 12:26 AM CST)

> "I'd like to see a list of the names of diseases where the letters would have been changed one time with translation because of the lack of the Roman letters because they were being used elsewhere as numbers."

## Short answer first — the hypothesis, tested, before the list

**The general hypothesis BREAKS; the narrow version HOLDS.**

- Roman numeral letters were **not** removed from the alphabet, and disease names were **not** generally renamed to avoid them. The seven numeral letters — **I (1), V (5), X (10), L (50), C (100), D (500), M (1000)** — all still appear in disease names today (see §1). A reader tells numeral from letter by context and position, not by changing the disease's spelling.
- What **did** change disease spellings, once, in translation, is a different shortage: classical Latin had **23 letters, not 26**. It had no **J**, no **U** (as a separate letter), and no **W**. One letter did two jobs — **I** was both vowel I and consonant J; **V** was both vowel U and consonant V/W. When those pairs split (largely in the 1500s–1600s, in print), disease names written with the old single letter were respelled with the new split letter — **one letter changed, one time**, in exactly the sense he asked for.
- A second, separate one-letter change: Greek → Latin transliteration, where Greek **kappa (K)** became Latin **C** — because classical Latin used C for the K sound in most words. That is a translation change, not a numeral change, and the list keeps it in its own section so the two causes are never merged.

So the list below is built on the documented causes, with the numeral-avoidance idea tested and shown where it stops.

## §1. The numeral letters themselves — proof they were NOT avoided

Each numeral letter, with a disease name that still carries it today. If numeral use had forced a rename, none of these would survive in this spelling.

| Numeral | Value | Disease name carrying it today | Letter's job in the name |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 1 | **Diabetes, meningitis, arthritis, hepatitis** | Vowel, in every -itis name |
| V | 5 | **Virus diseases generally; variola (smallpox), varicella (chickenpox), vaccinia** | Consonant V, initial |
| X | 10 | **Xeroderma, xanthoma, xerophthalmia** (Greek *xeros*, dry; *xanthos*, yellow) | Initial X, Greek root kept |
| L | 50 | **Lupus, malaria** (Latin *malus*, bad + *aria*, air), **melanoma** | L in lupus, malaria, melanoma |
| C | 100 | **Cancer, carcinoma, cyst** (Greek *kystis* via Latin C) | C for the old K sound |
| D | 500 | **Diabetes, dermatitis, dysentery** | Initial D, Greek *dia-* / *derma* |
| M | 1000 | **Malaria, melanoma, meningitis, myalgia** | Initial M, Latin *malus* / Greek *mys* |

Verdict on the numeral cause: **BREAKS generally.** Numerals and letters coexisted; context separated them. No disease in this section was renamed because its letter was also a number.

## §2. The real one-letter changes — the U–V split

Classical Latin wrote U and V as one letter, **V** (in inscriptions, V; in handwriting, u/v by position). Modern spelling splits them: **U** for the vowel, **V** for the consonant. Disease and anatomy names that were written with V as a vowel now carry U — one letter changed, one time, in the vowel position.

| Classical / inscription form | Modern form | Letter changed | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VLCVS** | **ulcus → ulcer** | V → U (first letter, vowel) | Latin *ulcus*, sore, ulcer. The V was a U all along in sound. |
| **VRTICARIA** (from *urtica*, nettle) | **urticaria** | V → U | Nettle-rash disease; nettle sting resemblance, not a numeral change. |
| **VVEA** (from *uva*, grape) | **uvea → uveitis** | V → U (first letter) | The uveal tract, grape-like in colour; -itis = inflammation. |
| **VREMIA** (from *ur-* + *-aemia*, urine in blood) | **uraemia / uremia** | V → U | Blood-urea condition; modern US spelling drops the ae as well (a second, later change — see §4). |
| **VLCER** variants, **VTERVS** | **uterus** (uterine disease names follow) | V → U | Womb; all uterine disease names inherit the split. |
| **VACCINIA** (from *vacca*, cow) | **vaccinia** (unchanged) | None — V here is consonantal | Control case: V as a consonant did NOT change. The split only changed vowel-V to U, which is why *vaccinia* kept its V while *ulcus* lost its V. This control is what proves the rule is the U–V split, not numeral avoidance. |

## §3. The real one-letter changes — the I–J split

Classical Latin wrote I and J as one letter, **I**. Modern spelling splits them: **I** for the vowel, **J** for the consonant (the Y/W-like sound). Disease and anatomy names that were written with I as a consonant now carry J.

| Classical form | Modern form | Letter changed | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IEIUNUM** (from *ieiunus*, fasting, empty) | **jejunum → jejunitis** | I → J (first letter, consonant) | Middle small intestine, empty after death; -itis = inflammation. |
| **IVGULUM / IVGULARIS** (throat) | **jugular → jugular vein thrombosis** | I → J | Throat vein; all jugular disease names inherit the split. |
| **ICTERVS** (Greek *ikteros*, yellow) | **icterus** (kept) vs. **jaundice** (French *jaunisse*, yellow, via J) | I → J in the French/English branch only | Control case with a fork: the learned Latin form kept I (*icterus*), the vernacular form took J (*jaundice*). Same disease, two spellings, split by language route — not by numerals. |
| **IVVENIS** (young) | **juvenile** (in juvenile arthritis, juvenile diabetes — old usage) | I → J | Age adjective, not a disease root, but it rides in disease names. |
| **IVS** (broth, juice — in *ius*) | **jus** (rare, in old pharmacy) | I → J | Pharmacy Latin; shows the split outside disease names too. |

## §4. The real one-letter changes — Greek → Latin transliteration (K → C, and the ae/oe simplifications)

These are translation changes, one letter (or one digraph) at a time, but their cause is alphabet difference between Greek and Latin, not numerals. Kept separate from §§2–3.

| Greek form | Latin / modern form | Letter changed | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **karkinos** (crab) | **cancer, carcinoma** | K → C | Tonight's cancer row, pluribus file. Crab-shape metaphor, carried by C. |
| **kystis** (bladder, sac) | **cyst, cystitis** | K → C | Sac diseases; -itis = inflammation. |
| **kephalē** (head) | **cephal-** (cephalgia, cephalitis — old, encephalitis) | K → C, ph kept | Head diseases; *encephalitis* keeps the Greek *en-* + *kephal-* inside Latin spelling. |
| **kardia** (heart) | **cardiac, carditis, cardiomyopathy** | K → C | Heart diseases; C is 100 as a numeral and still kept — again, numeral avoidance breaks. |
| **haemophilia, anaemia, diarrhoea, gonorrhoea, oedema** | **hemophilia, anemia, diarrhea, gonorrhea, edema** (US) | ae → e, oe → e (one digraph, one letter lost) | Later, American spelling simplification — a second wave of one-letter changes, centuries after the U–V/I–J splits. British forms keep ae/oe. Same disease, two spellings, by country. |

## §5. The archaic Greek numeral letters — the one place where "used as numbers" DID remove a letter

There is one genuine case where a letter survived *only* as a numeral and vanished from words — but it is Greek, not Roman, and it predates disease naming in Latin:

- Greek **digamma (ϝ, value 6), koppa (ϙ, 90), sampi (ϡ, 900)** were dropped from the Greek alphabet as letters and kept only as numerals. Words that once began with digamma (the W sound) lost their first letter in later Greek — e.g. *woinos* → *oinos* (wine), *wergon* → *ergon* (work).
- **Disease-name consequence: none directly documented.** No disease name in his tables is built on a digamma-initial root, and no disease was renamed because of digamma/koppa/sampi. This section exists so the file does not overclaim: the "letter lost because it became a numeral" phenomenon is real, but its home is archaic Greek, not Roman disease translation, and it contributes no row to §§2–4.
- Tonight's earlier Gemini V/Z note belongs here too: Gemini corrected **V = 5, not 10** in Roman numerals (X = 10), and named digamma, koppa, sampi as the archaic Greek numeral letters. That correction is carried forward, not re-asked.

## §6. Verdicts, in his terms

- **"Letters changed one time with translation": HOLDS, for §§2–4.** U→V-split (V→U for vowels), I→J-split (I→J for consonants), K→C transliteration, ae/oe→e simplification — each is a one-letter (or one-digraph) change, once, in translation or in print standardisation.
- **"Because of the lack of Roman letters because they were being used elsewhere as numbers": BREAKS as the cause.** The lack was not numeral reservation — it was that classical Latin had 23 letters (no J, no separate U, no W) and that Greek and Latin alphabets differed. Numeral letters I, V, X, L, C, D, M all survive in disease names today (§1), which a numeral-avoidance rule would forbid.
- **Sharpened statement:** *Disease names changed one letter at a time when Latin split one letter into two (V into U/V, I into J/I), and when Greek words were Latinized (K into C) — not because numerals stole the letters, but because the alphabet itself grew new letters and translators carried Greek sounds into Latin ones.*

## My addition (labeled mine)

For the booklet, teach the control cases first, not the changed cases: *vaccinia* kept its V (consonant) while *ulcus* lost its V (vowel), and *icterus* kept its I while *jaundice* took a J. Students who see what did *not* change will understand the split rule in one glance — a letter changes only in the position where its job changed, and nowhere else.
