# Theory — Outside Vector to Inside Vector, Mosquito Bites, and Who Gets Surrounded (2026-11-02)

**Origin (his account):** Douglas, Nov 2, 2026. His great-grandfather's diagnosis — supposedly jungle rot, doctors said malaria, attributed by his great-grandfather to trudging in the swamps in the Philippines in World War Two — is part of what inspired Douglas to ask whether there is a connection from the outside vector to the inside vector, and whether all of us are infected from mosquito bites, with a portion of the population managing to stay away from those things and a large population always surrounded by them because of the way nature is. ## Postulates, split

### P1 — There is a connection from the outside vector to the inside vector
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS, in the vector sense.** A vector, by definition, carries a pathogen from outside the body to inside it: a mosquito takes a blood meal, and a pathogen it carries can enter the new host's bloodstream. That is the outside-to-inside bridge. The bridge is the bite plus the pathogen; the bite alone, without a pathogen, is not an infection.
- **Simply put:** a mosquito can be a doorway. Something has to walk through the doorway for there to be an infection.

### P2 — All of us are infected from mosquito bites
**His question, tested as stated.**
- **Verdict: BREAKS as a universal.** Not every mosquito bite infects, and not every mosquito can transmit a given pathogen. Transmission needs a competent vector species, a pathogen present in that mosquito, and a susceptible host. Many bites are from mosquitoes carrying nothing transmissible to humans, in places where the pathogen does not circulate. Malaria, specifically, is transmitted by *Anopheles* mosquitoes carrying *Plasmodium* parasites — not by mosquitoes in general, and not by every bite.
- **HOLDS rewritten:** all of us are *exposed* to mosquito bites to some degree, and a portion of bites worldwide do transmit disease. Exposure is universal in degree; infection is not universal in fact.
- **Simply put:** everybody gets bitten sometimes. Not everybody gets sick from a bite.

### P3 — A portion of the population manages to stay away, and a large population is always surrounded, because of the way nature is
**His statement, tested.**
- **Verdict: HOLDS as an exposure gradient, not as two fixed kinds of people.** Exposure varies by geography (tropics and swamp/lowland areas vs. cold or high areas), season, housing (screens, walls, bed nets), occupation (field, swamp, forest work vs. indoor work), and time outdoors at dusk and night. The same person can move between the "away" group and the "surrounded" group by moving, by season, or by housing.
- **Foothold from his inspiration:** swamp trudging in the Philippines in wartime is a high-exposure setting on every one of those axes — warm climate, standing water, outdoor living, night exposure. That makes his great-grandfather's attribution *plausible as an exposure story*, without proving which diagnosis was correct.
- **Simply put:** nature doesn't deal bites evenly. Where you live, work, and sleep decides how many doorways are open.

### P4 — Jungle rot and malaria as one connected showing
**Implied by the inspiration, tested separately because the two names must not be collapsed.**
- **Verdict: BREAKS as one disease, HOLDS as one high-exposure setting producing two different problems.** "Jungle rot" in general usage refers to skin problems of wet tropical service — fungal skin infection and immersion-related skin breakdown — while malaria is a blood parasite transmitted by mosquitoes. They share the setting (wet, tropical, outdoor, wartime Philippines) but not the cause, the organism, or the route. A person in that setting could have both, either, or neither, and one does not prove the other.
- **Flagged, not resolved:** which condition his great-grandfather actually had, and whether both names were used for one condition at the time, stays [GAP] — his account, his uncertainty, and no record checked here.

## Combined statement (his thought + the record)
The outside vector connects to the inside vector at the bite: a mosquito can carry a pathogen from the environment into the blood. That bridge is real, but it is not universal infection — it needs the right mosquito, the right pathogen, and the right exposure. Nature sorts people not into the bitten and the unbitten, but along a gradient of how surrounded they are, by place, season, work, and shelter. His great-grandfather's Philippines story is the human version of the high end of that gradient, and the reason the question was asked in the first place.

## My addition (mine, for his approval)
Name the gradient, don't just name the groups: place, season, work, shelter, and time of day are the five dials that move a person along it.
