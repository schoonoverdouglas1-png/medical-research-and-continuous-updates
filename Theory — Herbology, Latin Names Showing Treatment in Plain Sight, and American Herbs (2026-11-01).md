## Split postulates

### P1. Latin names show treatment — the *officinalis* label [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **Officinalis = pharmacy plant [D]:** "The genus name *Salvia* is derived from the Latin 'salvus' (= healthy, whole, safe, saved), and thus means 'the healing one.' The epithet *officinalis* suggests that it is an old medicinal plant, because 'officina' is the salesroom of a pharmacy, and 'officinalis' means commonly used in the pharmacy." The same root appears across the shelf: *Salvia officinalis* (sage), *Valeriana officinalis* (valerian, from Latin *valere*, to be strong/healthy), *Borago officinalis* (borage), *Rosmarinus officinalis* (rosemary). The epithet is a use label, printed in the name itself.
2. **Genus names also encode healing [D]:** *Salvia* from *salvus/salvere*, "to be in good health, to cure from the disease"; *Borago* per Linnaeus from *cor* (heart) + *ago* (to act), "from its use in medicine as a heart sedative" — derivation flagged uncertain in the source itself, carried here as uncertain, not as settled etymology.
3. **Limit [D]:** *officinalis* says "this plant was kept in the pharmacy," not "this plant cures X." It is a category label (medicinal stock), not a prescription. Reading it as a specific treatment overreaches; reading it as "treatment is in the name" at the category level holds.
**Verdict: HOLDS at the category level — Latin *officinalis* and healing genera literally label plants as medicine; BREAKS as a specific-treatment code.**
**Simply put:** When a plant's Latin name ends in *officinalis*, that's the old pharmacy's stamp on it: "we kept this one in the shop because it was medicine." The name says *that* it was medicine, not exactly *what* it cured.

### P2. Disease names live inside plant names — the Doctrine of Signatures [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **Lung, liver, eye, spleen in the names [D]:** "Lungwort (*Pulmonaria officinalis*) was used for pulmonary infections as its spotty leaves resemble a diseased lung"; "Liverwort (*Hepatica* species) was used to treat liver ailments due again to the appearance of the leaves"; "Eyebright (*Euphrasia rostkoviana*) was used for eye infections since the flowers were thought to look like a healthy eye"; "Spleenwort (*Asplenium trichomanes*) was used to treat the spleen because the spores on the back of the fronds resemble a spleen". *Pulmonaria* = *pulmo* (lung); *Hepatica* = *hepar* (liver); the disease organ is in the genus name.
2. **The system behind it [D]:** "The Doctrine of Signatures — *Signatura Rerum*, the signature of things" — "a plant that resembled a liver treated the liver. A flower that looked like an eye healed the eyes", formalized by Paracelsus in the 16th century and codified by Culpeper and Coles. Color signatures joined shape: "yellow-sapped plants for jaundice, red-bleeding roots for blood disorders" — the same color logic as the algae pigment theory (same hour).
3. **The doctrine is not a reliable treatment guide [D]:** "There is no biological mechanism by which a plant's appearance would reliably indicate its medicinal properties", and the doctrine's own history includes harmful misses — birthwort, used for pregnancy because of its uterus resemblance, "is carcinogenic and very damaging to the kidneys, owing to its aristolochic acid content". Liverwort's case is the honest middle: "no significant hepatoprotective activity has been demonstrated. The name persists long after the rationale has faded".
**Verdict: HOLDS as history — disease/organ names and signature shapes really do encode traditional treatment claims; BREAKS as medicine — resemblance does not prove efficacy, and sometimes it misleads dangerously.**
**Simply put:** Old herbalists named plants after the body part they looked like — lungwort for lungs, liverwort for liver, eyebright for eyes. That really is treatment written into the name. But looking like a liver doesn't mean it heals a liver, and following the look alone once led people to a plant that damages kidneys. The name is a clue to history, not a proof of cure.

### P3. It was hidden in plain sight [H]
**Test:**
1. **Hidden by reading, not by secrecy [LOGIC]:** the names were never secret — they are printed on every supplement label, every herbal, every flora. What changed is how we read them: modern readers read a binomial as taxonomy (genus + species, identification only), while older readers read it as a use label (pharmacy stock, organ signature). The hiding is a reading shift, not a conspiracy, and the file keeps it that way — no hidden-hand claim is made or needed.
2. **Plain sight, verified [D]:** *Salvia officinalis* is on sage products today; *Pulmonaria officinalis* is on lungwort products today. Anyone who knows *officina* = pharmacy and *pulmo* = lung can read the treatment claim off the label without any special access. That is "plain sight" in the strict sense.
3. **What plain sight does not give [D]:** it does not give dosage, safety, or proof. The label's Latin gives identity and traditional category; modern supplement regulation keeps treatment claims separate from identity for exactly that reason. A book that shows the names must say this on every page, or it turns a history book into a prescription book.
**Verdict: HOLDS as a reading claim — the treatment labels were public all along, in Latin, on the label; BREAKS as a secrecy claim — nothing was hidden by anyone, only unread in the old way.**
**Simply put:** Nobody hid these treatments. They were written on the bottle in Latin the whole time — we just stopped reading Latin as "this is medicine for this" and started reading it as just a plant's ID number. Your book's job is to teach the old reading again, not to expose a secret.

### P4. American herbs and supplements follow the same Latin logic — partially [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **American signature names, in common names [D]:** goldenseal is "Named for its rich golden color", *Hydrastis canadensis*, "a perennial plant native to eastern North America" — color (golden root) + place (*canadensis*, of Canada/North America) + traditional uses (Cherokee wash for cuts and wounds, eye wash, mouth sores; Iroquois tea for cough, liver disorders, fever). The color-signature logic is present in the common name, not in the Latin genus.
2. **American Latin names encode shape and place, not treatment [D]:** *Echinacea* = Greek *echinos* (hedgehog/sea-urchin), the spiky conehead — shape, not disease. *Hydrastis* = Greek *hydor* (water), habitat, not disease. *Echinacea* spp. and goldenseal traditional uses come from Native American practice (Lakota poultice for wounds/burns/bites; Cheyenne root for toothache/colds; Cherokee goldenseal wash) — knowledge carried in tribal practice and common names, not encoded in the binomial. For American herbs, the Latin often says *where* and *what it looks like*; the treatment lives in the people's use record.
3. **Supplements today [D]:** a modern supplement label prints the Latin binomial for identity and standardization (which species, which part), and treatment claims, where allowed, are printed separately as structure/function statements. So the Latin on a supplement bottle is doing the *officinalis* job (identity as a medicinal plant) and the signature job only where the common name carries it (goldenseal, lungwort). The book must keep those two jobs apart.
**Verdict: HOLDS partial — American herbs carry the same color/place/shape naming logic, and the *officinalis*/signature logic where it applies; BREAKS universal — for the flagship American herbs (echinacea, goldenseal), the Latin binomial itself does not name the treatment.**
**Simply put:** For American herbs, the Latin name usually tells you what it looks like or where it grows — spiky conehead, water plant from Canada — while the treatment story lives with the people who used it and in the common name, like goldenseal's golden root. Same family of clues, different place in the name.

### P5. The book project: show it, honestly [H, his project]
**Structure proposed (mine, labeled, for his approval):** each entry in three columns, never two: (1) Latin name, broken into roots, American first, Latin second; (2) traditional treatment claim the name encodes, with its source (signature, *officinalis*, tribal use, folk name); (3) documented activity today — proven, partial, none, or harmful — with the counterexamples kept in (birthwort, liverwort's faded rationale). A book with only columns 1 and 2 would be a prescription book in disguise; a book with all three is a history book that teaches the old reading without repeating its mistakes.
**Verdict: The project HOLDS as a book — the material is real, public, and teachable; it stands or falls on column 3 being kept in every entry.**
**Simply put:** Your book can absolutely show this — name, what the name claimed, and what we actually know now, side by side, every time. Leave out the third column and it's just old claims again. Keep it in and it's a real teaching book.

## My labeled addition (mine, not his)

**A one-page decoder for the book's front matter.** Four Latin keys, each with one worked example: *officinalis* = pharmacy stock (sage); *pulmo-/hepat-* = organ signature (lungwort/liverwort); color = function signature (yellow jaundice, red blood, golden goldenseal); place/shape = American naming (canadensis, echinos). Four keys, and the reader can decode any label in the book without the author standing over their shoulder. That decoder is the "plain sight" made usable.
