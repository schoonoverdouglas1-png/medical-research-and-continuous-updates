## His dictation, verbatim

> "Overburden equal swelling, swelling equals encephalitis and elephantitis and that cross references with the byproduct of a sick person with the showing of meningococcal pneumonia and streptococcus pneumonia both.:"

## Split postulates

### P1. Overburden equals swelling [H]
**Test:**
1. **Overburden → swelling [D]:** a heavy microbial or inflammatory load produces edema — fluid leaking from vessels, cells swelling, tissue enlarging. Inflammatory swelling is a standard consequence of overburden in the load sense.
2. **Swelling without overburden [D]:** swelling also occurs without a microbial overburden — allergy, trauma, venous or lymphatic blockage, kidney or heart failure. Overburden is one cause of swelling, not its identity.
3. **Overburden without swelling [D]:** a high viral or bacterial load can exist with little visible swelling (bloodstream infection, early pneumonia, latent carriage). Load and swelling track together often, not always.
**Verdict: HOLDS as a frequent consequence (overburden → swelling); BREAKS as an equation (overburden = swelling).**
**Simply put:** Too much going on in a tissue often makes it swell. But swelling can happen without germs, and germs can pile up without obvious swelling. They're linked, not identical.

### P2. Swelling equals encephalitis [H]
**Test:**
1. **Encephalitis includes swelling [D]:** "Encephalitis is an inflammation (irritation and swelling) of the brain, usually caused by infections." "The brain tissue swells (cerebral edema), which may cause destruction of nerve cells".
2. **Encephalitis is more than swelling [D]:** encephalitis is inflammation of brain parenchyma with neuronal dysfunction, cell invasion, and often destruction — swelling is one component, and the dangerous part is where it happens (inside a rigid skull, raising pressure), not swelling in general.
3. **Swelling elsewhere is not encephalitis [D]:** a swollen ankle, lung, or limb is not encephalitis. Site plus inflammation plus brain dysfunction are all required.
**Verdict: HOLDS as "encephalitis involves brain swelling"; BREAKS as "swelling = encephalitis."**
**Simply put:** Encephalitis is brain swelling with brain damage — not just any swelling. A swollen leg isn't encephalitis, and encephalitis isn't just swelling.

### P3. Swelling equals elephantiasis [H, "elephantitis" his word]
**Test:**
1. **Elephantiasis involves swelling [D]:** "Elephantiasis, also known as lymphatic filariasis, is a chronic disease that primarily affects the lymphatic system. The disease is characterized by the abnormal swelling and enlargement of body parts, most commonly the limbs and genitalia."
2. **Elephantiasis is a specific swelling [D]:** it is chronic lymphatic obstruction — "Elephantiasis results from the obstruction of or damage to the lymphatic vessels, which drain excess fluids from tissues." — most often by filarial parasitic worms (*Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia* spp.), over years, with skin thickening and hardening. It is not acute inflammatory swelling, and it is not brain swelling.
3. **The shared element [H→D]:** encephalitis and elephantiasis share exactly one element — swelling — and differ in site (brain vs. limb/genital), time course (acute days vs. chronic years), mechanism (inflammation/edema vs. lymphatic obstruction/fibrosis), and cause (usually virus vs. parasitic worm). Sharing an element is a cross-reference, not an identity.
**Verdict: HOLDS as "both are swelling diseases"; BREAKS as "swelling = elephantiasis" and BREAKS as "encephalitis = elephantiasis."**
**Simply put:** Elephantiasis is long-term swelling from blocked lymph drains, usually from a worm, making a leg or other part huge and thick-skinned over years. Encephalitis is fast brain swelling over days. Same word "swelling," completely different diseases.

### P4. This cross-references with the byproduct of a sick person, shown as meningococcal pneumonia and pneumococcal pneumonia, both [H]
**Test, three ways:**
1. **Both organisms cause both syndromes [D]:** "about 80% of the cases [of adult bacterial meningitis] are now caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis". Pneumococcus is the "Most common cause of community-acquired pneumonia, bacterial meningitis". Meningococcus causes meningitis and septicemia primarily, with pneumonia as a less common presentation — so "meningococcal pneumonia" is real but rarer than pneumococcal pneumonia, and the two are not symmetric in frequency.
2. **Both are encapsulated diplococci [D]:** "Examples include Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis" as encapsulated diplococci, and "All three of these pathogens possess a capsule that protects them from phagocytosis" (with *Haemophilus influenzae* as the third classic). Capsule logic is the genuine cross-reference — the same defense, different capsule chemistry (pneumococcal numbered serotypes vs. meningococcal letter serogroups, which do not translate into each other, per Oct 28–31 files).
3. **Byproduct / showing [H→D]:** a sick person's byproduct — sputum, blood, cerebrospinal fluid — can *show* either organism, and a multiplex panel can show both if both are present. Showing is not causation (carriage shows without disease, per his Oct 31 carrier thesis), and showing both is not the same disease. The cross-reference holds at the level of "same sample, same syndrome, same capsule defense" and breaks at "same disease."
**Verdict: HOLDS as a cross-reference — two encapsulated diplococci, both causing meningitis and pneumonia, both readable from a sick person's samples; BREAKS as an identity between the two pneumonias.**
**Simply put:** Pneumococcus and meningococcus are cousins in shape and armor — both round pairs with a capsule that hides them from your immune system, both able to cause meningitis and pneumonia. Pneumococcus is the usual pneumonia culprit; meningococcus usually causes meningitis and only sometimes pneumonia. Same family trick, different habits.
