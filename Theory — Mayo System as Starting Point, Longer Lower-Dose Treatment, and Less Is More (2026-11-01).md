## Split postulates

- **P1:** Even Mayo Clinic's computer system is a starting point for treatments, not the endpoint.
- **P2:** Original physicians from 50-plus years ago knew they had to treat sicker patients longer.
- **P3:** Sometimes successful outcome requires longer treatments with lower dosages.
- **P4:** A mouse study he watched/read online compared vancomycin with a newer drug, working with the strengths and dosages of the medicine.
- **P5:** In some circumstances less is more, and sometimes more is less.
- **P6:** Sometimes more-or-less is perfect, depending on the person and the exact balance of things that have to be done to make things work.

**Simply put.** Guidelines and computers give you the starting dose and the starting course. The old doctors knew the sicker you were, the longer you needed — and sometimes the answer isn't a bigger dose, it's a smaller dose for longer. Less can beat more, more can beat less, and the perfect amount depends on the person and the balance you're trying to hit.

## Tests

### P1 — Mayo computer system as a starting point
**HOLDS [H→D].** Clinical decision-support systems, order sets, and guidelines at large systems (Mayo included, in general terms) are designed as starting points: they encode the standard regimen for a standard patient, and the treating clinician is expected to adjust for the actual patient — allergies, kidney and liver function, organism and susceptibility, severity, and response [D, general]. A system that *were* the endpoint, with no clinician adjustment, would be unsafe by design, because no two patients match the standard patient exactly. His framing — "starts as a starting point" — is therefore the correct way to describe such a system, and it connects to tonight's earlier doctrine: the doctor's interpretation, not the system output alone, is where care happens.

### P2 — Old physicians knew to treat sicker patients longer
**HOLDS partial, BREAKS as a universal.**
- Holds: for many severe, deep, or slow-clearing infections, longer therapy is genuinely the historical and current practice — endocarditis, osteomyelitis, tuberculosis, and deep abscesses are treated for weeks to months, precisely because sicker, deeper infections clear slowly [D, general]. Physicians 50-plus years ago, working before many modern diagnostics, learned this from observation and relapse: stop too early, and the sickest patients relapsed.
- Breaks universally: modern evidence has also *shortened* therapy for many infections where sicker patients were once treated longer by default — community-acquired pneumonia, urinary tract infection, intra-abdominal infection, and others now have trial-supported short courses that are non-inferior to longer ones [D, general]. So "sicker = longer" holds for deep/severe infections and breaks as a blanket rule: the right duration depends on the infection, the site, and the response, not on sickness alone.

### P3 — Longer treatments with lower dosages for successful outcome
**HOLDS in specific pharmacologic senses, BREAKS as a general rule.**
- Holds, first sense — **time-dependent killing:** for beta-lactam antibiotics, what matters most is the *time* the drug concentration stays above the organism's MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration), not the peak height. Prolonged or continuous infusion — effectively, a lower peak sustained longer — can therefore outperform a short high peak for the same total dose [D, general]. That is a documented "longer, not higher" success.
- Holds, second sense — **toxicity avoidance:** vancomycin (see P4) is the classic example: higher doses and higher troughs increase kidney injury risk, so the successful regimen is often the *lowest effective* exposure that still hits the target, monitored by levels — longer at a safe level beats shorter at a toxic one [D, general].
- Breaks generally: lowering the dose below the effective exposure for a given organism risks treatment failure and resistance selection [D, general]. "Lower" only helps when it is still *enough* — lower than toxic, not lower than effective. The distinction between those two "lowers" is the whole pharmacology, and this dictation does not draw it explicitly, so it is drawn here.

### P4 — Mouse study: vancomycin vs. a newer drug, strengths and dosages
**Filed as his account of a study he watched/read [H]; the study itself is [GAP] — not identified, not verified.**
- What can be said generally [D]: mouse (murine) infection models are a standard preclinical step for comparing an established drug (vancomycin is a frequent comparator for Gram-positive infections, especially MRSA) with a newer agent, varying dose and exposure to find the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target (AUC/MIC, time above MIC) that predicts efficacy.
- What cannot be said: which newer drug, which infection model, which doses, or what the result was — none of that is named in this dictation, and no study is cited. No result from an unnamed mouse study is claimed here, and mouse results do not translate directly to human dosing: mice metabolize drugs faster, and human doses are set by human trials, not by mouse doses.
- If he supplies the drug name, the model, or a link, the study can be looked up and tested specifically. Until then it stays as his account of having seen such a study.

### P5 — Less is more, sometimes more is less
**HOLDS [D, general].**
- Less is more: de-escalation (narrowing once cultures return), short courses where trials support them, and dose reduction in kidney impairment — all documented cases where less drug gives equal or better outcomes with less harm [D].
- More is less: the converse is also documented — more drug can mean more toxicity, more resistance selection, more *Clostridioides difficile* risk, and worse outcomes, not better ones [D]. "More" is not a synonym for "more effective" once the effective exposure is reached.
- The two halves together are the therapeutic-window principle: below the window, the drug fails; above it, it harms; inside it, it works. Both slogans are true, at opposite edges of the window.

### P6 — More-or-less perfect, depending on the person and the exact balance
**HOLDS [H→D].** This is the individualization principle, stated in his balance language: the perfect regimen depends on the person (weight, kidney/liver function, age, immune status, allergies), the organism (species, MIC, resistance), the site (blood, lung, bone, meninges — penetration differs), and the response over time [D, general]. "Exact balance of stuff" is his phrase for what pharmacology calls the exposure–response balance: enough drug, at the right site, for the right time, without excess toxicity. No single dose is perfect for every person, which is why P1's starting-point system must be adjusted by a clinician — and why P3's "longer, lower" can be perfect for one person and wrong for another.

## Combined statement (his thought + tested thought)
His picture survives testing in its core and narrows at its edges: a computer system, however good, is a starting point, and the old doctors' observation — sicker, deeper infections need longer — is real, but modern trials have also shortened many courses, so sickness alone does not set duration. "Longer with lower dosages" is genuinely successful in the time-dependent and toxicity-avoidance senses, and genuinely dangerous if "lower" means below effective exposure. Less is more and more is less are both true, at opposite edges of the therapeutic window, and the perfect amount is the one balanced to the person, the organism, and the site — which is exactly why the system can start the treatment but cannot finish it. The mouse study behind part of this remains unnamed and untested, awaiting a drug name or a link.

## My addition (labeled mine)
For the teaching booklet, draw the therapeutic window as a staircase of its own: too low (fails), just right (works), too high (harms) — and put the "longer, lower" idea on the middle step, not the bottom one. Students who see that longer-lower means *sustained enough*, not *less than enough*, will not mistake under-dosing for gentleness.
