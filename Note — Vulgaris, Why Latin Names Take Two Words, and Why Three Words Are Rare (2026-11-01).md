## 1. Vulgaris — what the word means [D]

*Vulgaris* is a Latin adjective meaning **common, ordinary, of the common people** — from *vulgus*, the crowd, the common people. In a scientific name it is the *specific epithet* (the second word), and it almost always means "the common one" of its genus, as opposed to a rarer or more distinctive relative.

Examples in his own fields and nearby:
- *Beta vulgaris* — the common beet.
- *Plantago major* is a near-neighbour pattern (major = greater); *vulgaris* plays the same "common" role in dozens of genera.
- In medicine, *vulgaris* appears in common names turned Latin: acne *vulgaris* (common acne), lupus *vulgaris* (a form of cutaneous tuberculosis, "common lupus" in the old sense), verruca *vulgaris* (common wart).
- The pattern to teach: the epithet is an adjective describing *which* member of the genus — common, greater, smaller, hairy, smooth — not a second noun.

## 2. Why two words, not one — the original reasoning [D]

Before Linnaeus, a species was described by a *polynomial*: a long Latin phrase, sometimes a whole sentence, listing its distinguishing features. That phrase was the name, and it changed whenever someone found a better description.

Linnaeus (botany 1753, zoology 1758) replaced the phrase with a **binomial**: exactly two words.

- **Word 1 — genus (capitalized):** the group the organism belongs to. *Mycobacterium, Streptococcus, Neisseria, Homo.*
- **Word 2 — specific epithet (lowercase):** the adjective (or noun in apposition) that distinguishes this species *within* that genus. *tuberculosis, pneumoniae, meningitidis, vulgaris, sapiens.*

The original reasoning, in one line: **one word cannot distinguish, and a sentence cannot be remembered.** A genus alone (*Mycobacterium*) names dozens of species; an epithet alone (*vulgaris*) appears in hundreds of genera and names nothing by itself. Together, and only together, they name exactly one species — *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* is one organism, *Beta vulgaris* is another, and neither word alone does the job. Latin was chosen because it was already the shared scholarly language of Europe, it was not any one nation's living language (so no nation owned it), and its grammar was fixed enough to be stable across centuries.

That is why his earlier naming doctrine (American first, Latin second) and the binomial system fit together rather than fight: the American common name tells a person what it is in their language, and the Latin binomial tells a scientist exactly which organism, in a form that does not change when the common name does.

## 3. Why three words are rare — and where they do exist [D]

His observation is correct as a general rule: **formal species names in medicine and science are two words, and three-word species names are rare.** The reason is the same economy: if two words already uniquely identify the species, a third word is unnecessary — and nomenclature codes actively discourage unnecessary words.

Three words appear, legitimately, in these places — and only these places:

**a) Subspecies / variety / form — the trinomial [D].**
When a species is split into recognizably distinct populations, a third word is added:
- Zoology (subspecies): *Homo sapiens sapiens* — genus, species, subspecies. Three words, each a rank.
- Botany (variety/form): *Beta vulgaris* var. *cicla* — with "var." marking the rank, so the full form is longer still.
- Bacteriology (subspecies): *Mycobacterium bovis* subsp. *bovis* — again, the rank word ("subsp.") is part of the full name, so the formal name is four tokens, not three.

The rule behind the rarity: a third word is only added when a *rank below species* needs naming. No subspecies, no third word.

**b) Author citations (not part of the name, but often printed with it) [D].**
*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (Zopf 1883) Lehmann & Neumann 1896 — the names in parentheses are the authors who described and recombined it, not a third descriptive word. Students often mistake an author for a third word; it is a credit, not a description.

**c) Multi-word anatomical, chemical, and disease phrases — which are not species names [D].**
Medicine is full of three-plus-word Latin *phrases* — *musculus biceps brachii*, *arteria coronaria sinistra*, *carcinoma in situ* — but those are descriptive anatomical or pathological phrases, not binomial species names, and they follow different rules (they describe a structure, not a species). So his claim holds for *species names* and breaks if extended to *all Latin medical terms*: three-word Latin phrases in anatomy are common; three-word Latin species names are rare.

**d) The tuberculosis complex as a near-miss [D, from tonight's TB file].**
*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex is three words in English usage, but "complex" is not a Latin epithet — it is an English grouping word for a set of closely related species (*M. tuberculosis, M. bovis, M. africanum,* etc.). It looks like a three-word Latin name and is not one.

## 4. Verdict on his dictation, postulate by postulate

- **"Latin as a term of two words describes a thing": HOLDS for species [D].** The binomial is the system, for the reason above: genus + epithet = one species, uniquely.
- **"It was decided that it took at least two words": HOLDS, with a date [D].** Decided by Linnaeus, 1753 (plants) and 1758 (animals), replacing polynomials. "At least two" is the right phrasing: two is the minimum for a species, and the system adds a third only for a lower rank.
- **"What is three words, there's more description": HOLDS [LOGIC].** A third word, when it appears, always adds a rank (subspecies, variety) and therefore more description — *Homo sapiens sapiens* says more than *Homo sapiens*, because it narrows to one population within the species.
- **"Not very many Latin terms in medicine or science have three words": HOLDS for species names, BREAKS for Latin phrases generally.** Species names: rare, by rule. Anatomical and pathological Latin phrases: common, by a different rule.

## 5. Combined statement (his thought + tested thought)

His instinct is right and it has a date: Latin species naming was *decided* — by Linnaeus, in the 1750s — to take two words because one word cannot distinguish and a sentence cannot be remembered, and *vulgaris* is the perfect example of the second word's job, saying "the common one" of its genus. Three words are rare for the same reason two words are the rule: a third word is only spent when a lower rank needs naming, and spending it otherwise would waste the economy the whole system was invented for. The apparent exceptions — subspecies, author names, anatomical phrases, the tuberculosis complex — are not exceptions to the binomial rule at all; they are different kinds of names, following different rules, that happen to sit beside it.

## My addition (labeled mine)

For the booklet, teach vulgaris first, before any other epithet: one slide, *Beta vulgaris* (common beet) beside *Beta* alone (a genus, many beets) and *vulgaris* alone (a word, hundreds of genera). Students who see that one slide will never again ask why a species needs two words — they will have seen one word fail twice, in opposite directions.
