## Split postulates

- **P1:** Disease is embedded within us, like colonization — the stuff is colonized there.
- **P2:** Once we're well, our DNA shares DNA with the viruses.
- **P3:** White blood cells have to have some type of connection at the chemical level with viruses, bacteria, and fungi, and with our blood and our tissues.

**Simply put.** Germs don't just visit us — they settle in, like colonists in a place. Even after we're well, pieces of old viruses are written into our own DNA. And the white blood cells are the go-betweens: they talk chemistry with germs, blood, and tissues alike.

## Tests

### P1 — Disease embedded as colonization
**HOLDS [H→D], with a distinction that matters.**

Colonization is a documented, central concept [D, general]: organisms can live on or in a person — skin, nose, gut, respiratory tract — without causing disease, and that colonizing population is the reservoir from which disease can arise when conditions change (immune suppression, a barrier break, a new strain, a balance shift — tonight's earlier balance file). Examples in his own work: pneumococcal carriage in the nasopharynx, *Moraxella* as a common upper-respiratory colonizer, *Candida* colonizing healthy people (the 63% figure from Nov 1).

The distinction: **colonization ≠ disease, and disease ≠ colonization.** A colonized person may never become ill, and a disease may be acute and cleared without lasting colonization. What holds is the embeddedness: the organisms that cause disease are often already *there*, in tissues and on surfaces, before disease starts — which is why his "embedded within us" framing is stronger than a framing where disease only ever arrives from outside. Latency is the deeper version of the same idea: herpesviruses, tuberculosis, and others persist in the body in a dormant state and can reactivate [D, general].

So P1 holds as: *the stuff is colonized there, and disease often grows out of what is already embedded* — and breaks only if read as "all disease is colonization," which would erase acute, externally acquired infections that never colonize first.

### P2 — Once we're well, our DNA shares DNA with the viruses
**HOLDS for endogenous retroviruses, BREAKS as a general rule for all viruses.**

The documented core [D]: about **8% of the human genome is composed of sequences with viral origin, namely human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs)** — relics of ancient infections that entered the primate germ line over the last ~100 million years and became stable, inherited elements. That is literal DNA sharing: viral sequences, written into human DNA, passed to every cell and to offspring.

The limits, which must be kept with the claim:
1. **Germ line vs. lifetime:** HERVs entered the *germ line* of ancestors, not the DNA of a person during their own illness and recovery. Getting well from a cold, a pneumonia, or a fungal infection does not write that virus into your inherited DNA. Retroviruses can integrate into *somatic* cells during a person's lifetime (HIV being the example), but that integration is in those cells, not in the DNA you pass on, and it is not true of most viruses at all.
2. **Most viruses do not integrate:** influenza, coronaviruses, pneumococcal bacteria, most fungi — none of these write themselves into human DNA as a normal part of infection. Their "sharing" with us is through immune memory (antibodies, T cells), not through shared DNA.
3. **"Once we're well" timing:** the HERV sharing predates any individual's wellness by millions of years. Wellness does not cause it; it is already there, well or ill.

So P2, sharpened: *our DNA already shares DNA with ancient viruses (HERVs, ~8%), whether we are well or ill — and that sharing is inherited, not acquired by getting well from an ordinary infection.* The well-state version breaks; the inherited version holds.

### P3 — White blood cells connected at the chemical level with viruses, bacteria, fungi, blood, and tissues
**HOLDS strongly [D].**

This is, in documented terms, the immune system's core job, and it operates exactly at the chemical level he imagines [D, general]:
- **Recognition is chemical:** white blood cells carry receptors (Toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors, C-type lectins, and others) that bind specific microbial chemical patterns — bacterial lipopolysaccharide and peptidoglycan, fungal β-glucans and mannans, viral RNA/DNA patterns. The cell does not "see" a bacterium; it binds its chemistry.
- **Response is chemical:** once bound, white blood cells release cytokines and chemokines (chemical signals that recruit other cells), produce antibodies (proteins that bind microbial chemistry), and perform phagocytosis (engulfing a microbe and digesting it chemically).
- **Blood and tissues are the medium:** white blood cells travel in blood, exit into tissues at chemical signals, and return signals to blood — so blood and tissue are not separate from the connection, they are where it happens. Inflammation itself is a chemical conversation between white blood cells, blood vessels, and tissue cells.
- **Across kingdoms:** the same white blood cell types (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, lymphocytes) handle viruses, bacteria, and fungi — with different receptors and different effectors for each, but through the same chemical-recognition principle. That is the commonality point from tonight's earlier meningitis file, restated at the cellular level: different coats, same chemical meeting place.

No part of P3 needs narrowing except one: "have to have" is an inference, but it is a sound one — without chemical recognition, white blood cells could not distinguish self from microbe, and the immune system as documented could not function. The connection is not imagined; it is the mechanism.

## Combined statement (his thought + tested thought)

His picture holds together, once the timing of P2 is corrected: disease is often already embedded as colonization, waiting on a balance shift; our DNA already carries ancient viral writing (HERVs, ~8%), inherited rather than earned by getting well; and white blood cells are the chemical go-betweens that make all of it work — binding bacterial, fungal, and viral chemistry, signalling through blood, and acting in tissues. Embeddedness, inherited viral DNA, and chemical immune connection are three faces of the same fact: we are not separate from the microbes we host, we are a system that contains them, carries their history in our genome, and talks to them in chemistry every day.
